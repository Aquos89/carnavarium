1. Introduction
Colombia's Caribbean coast is home to many of the country's most colorful and recognizable traditions.
Barranquilla Carnival is perhaps the most prominent example. It is the largest festival in the country,
received more than one million visitors in 2019 (Cotelco 2019), and perfectly reflects the highlights of
the Colombian Caribbean culture and at the same time the socioeconomic differences that characterize
the region, one of the most unequal in the country (UNDP 2021). Its music, costumes and dances have
been a seal of identity for different population niches, but it is also the object of appropriation by others
with more power in the social hierarchy. This has created a series of narratives and discourses that for
many years were considered unique and true, and that have forged different ways of understanding
this carnival.
Thanks to the processes of globalization, the Barranquilla Carnival is taking on new forms, as recent
ideas have gained strength and have come into tension with all those social, political and economic
dynamics of past decades that have been passed down from generation to generation. This paper
attempts to explain how narratives and representations have been consolidated creating different
discourses of the carnival, specifically on authenticity, and how today the tensions underlying their
coexistence are perceived in the music listened to and enjoyed by the members of a collective costume
troupe, called La Puntica No Má', a comparsa1 (actually, a collective costume troupe) of the Carnival of
Barranquilla.
Reviewing White's definitions of narratives, representations and discourses (1990), the main ideas on
authenticity (Salge Ferro 2014, 2018; Smith 2006, 2011, 2014; Kim, Oh, Lee & Lee 2018; Wesener 2017;
Wang 1999; Yi, Lin, Jin & Luo 2017) and Appadurai´s Five „Scapes“ of Globalization (1998), an attempt
is made to answer a simple question:

1.1. The Colombian Caribbean, Barranquilla and its Carnival
Talking about the Colombian Caribbean means understanding one of the most striking and diverse
regions of the country. It is located in the north of the country, being the region that borders the
Caribbean Sea. Meisel and Pérez (2006) speak of a great variety of ecosystems ranging from the deserts
of La Guajira peninsula to the great savannas of Córdoba and Sucre, passing through paradisiacal
beaches, many rivers such as Magdalena -navigable and considered the most important in the country-
, and the perpetual snows of the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta.
Understanding the people of this region of Colombia can also be very complex since it is a mixture of
people of different origins such as Spaniards, local indigenous people, Africans who arrived during the
colony as slaves, English, Italians, Germans, Jews, Arabs from countries such as Lebanon, Syria and
Jordan, Chinese, among many others (Zambrano Pantoja 2006). The interaction of all these people
located in different ecosystemic varieties forged in turn local identities that remain today, for example,
the Ribereños -the coastal people who live on the banks of the Magdalena River-, the Sabaneros -those
who live in the savannas of the western coast where cattle ranching is part of the landscape- and the
Guajiros -who live on the Guajira Peninsula and have to deal with the great desert that characterizes

this sub-region (Acevedo Merlano 2020). Despite the great diversity of cultural elements that
characterize this region, all its inhabitants are often referred to as Costeños (Acevedo Merlano 2020;
Agudelo 2013) or Caribbeans for all those who identify with the Caribbean culture, regardless of
whether they live in front of the sea or not, and which is associated with the way of being of the people
who live in the Caribbean Basin and includes people from Spanish-speaking, English-speaking, French-
speaking and Dutch-speaking countries (Acevedo Merlano 2020; Premdas 1996). The countries facing
this sea are usually called Caribbean countries, although in some cases only a part of their inhabitants
identify themselves as Caribbean, since only a part of their territory is economically, politically,
historically and culturally linked to this region, for example, the Colombian Caribbeans, the Floridians
in the United States and the yucatecans in Mexico. (See Appendix A).
All Costeños are seen by Colombians from other regions as cheerful and jocular people (Agudelo 2013;
Gómez Pino & Villadiego Díaz 2016). This perception is also given by the Costeños themselves and their
music to a large extent transmits this idea of happiness and celebration, which of course is taken
advantage of by the people of Barranquilla to promote their cultural events (Hernández Salgar 2014).
Barranquilla is a city of 1,115,490 inhabitants2, capital of Departmento de Atlántico [Department of
Atlántico], located also in the Colombian Caribbean (See Appendix B). Due to its strategic location on
the banks of the Magdalena River and facing the Caribbean Sea, the city became a meeting point for
different ethnic groups and migrants of different origins. For this reason, it has a vibrant mix of cultural,
economic and social influences that makes the city stand out for its diversity and vitality (Sourdis Nájera
2017).
Understanding Barranquilla always implies stopping at its biggest festival, the Carnival of Barranquilla,
which lasts four days and always ends one day before Ash Wednesday, a holy day for the Catholic faith.
During the time the carnival lasts there are parades, street parties, private parties in clubs and
commercial establishments, contests, and many concerts. In its parades it can be observed an endless
number of dances, with costumes of all kinds and very lively music, which are a consequence of a great
mixture of all the cultural influences that Barranquilla and the Colombian Caribbean region received
during the last centuries. For all the above mentioned, the Carnival of Barranquilla was declared
Intangible Heritage of Humanity by the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization
(UNESCO) in 20083.

1.2. Carnival music
However, all carnivals have elements in common, for example their origin in the Dionysian festivities,
which came from Greece to Rome, spreading throughout the territory of the Roman Empire as the
Saturnalian festivities (or simply as Saturnalia) until their introduction in the Middle Ages with the name
they are known today. (Covo Torres 1990; Friedemann 1985). During the Renaissance it spread
throughout the Christian world and then to other geographical contexts through conquests and
exchanges with Europe (Vignolo 2006). The celebratory and unbridled character inherited through the
ages, contrary to what the church dictated, also determines another of the core elements of the
carnival: its music. This is usually joyful, energetic, jocular, provocative, boisterous and danceable
music. Often, carnival songs and melodies focus on themes of celebration, fun, cultural identity and
social satire.
Carnival music varies widely according to locality and specific cultural tradition. It can include a mixture
of musical styles and genres, such as folk music, popular music, traditional music and dance music.
Some well-known examples of carnival music include samba in the carnival of Rio de Janeiro (Brazil),
Basler Trommeln music in Basel (Switzerland), calypso in the carnival of Trinidad and Tobago, marches
and polkas in the carnivals of Cologne (Germany) and Venice (Italy), and candombe in the carnival of
Montevideo (Uruguay). However, these carnivals do not always feature this music, which is considered
more folkloric, but also other more commercial musical genres known to non-local audiences.
Electronic music, for example, could not be absent in Notting Hill in London or Tenerife in Spain, but
neither in Gualeguaychú in Argentina or Salvador in Brazil.
In Barranquilla, precisely, the musical genre with which its carnival is most associated is cumbia (F.
Ochoa 2017). A rhythm that in turn has a characteristic dance and attire during carnival times. Musically
it is a genre that has a binary time, moderate tempo and is generally not so syncopated as other
Caribbean rhythms (See Appendix C). It is rather calm and lilting. In carnival times it is played with three
types of drums -alegre, llamador and tambora- and a minor percussion instrument which can be a
maraca or a guache, a cylindrical friction instrument. This instrumental format is called conjunto de
caña de millo, conjunto de pito de millo, conjunto de pito atravesao or grupo'e millo, due to its melodic
instrument of indigenous origin: the millo flute4 (F. Ochoa 2019; See Appendix D). Carnival cumbia and
the other music heard during this festival are in fact the result of multiple exchanges between the city,
the region, the country and the Caribbean basin. Along with the millo group, there are other types of
ensembles that participate in the carnival, these are:

• The parrandas vallenatas, with percussion instruments such as cajas vallenatas and

• Tropical orchestras, which maintain a format similar to that of Big Bands, composed of four or
five instrument sections: wind section (brass such as trumpets, trombones and saxophones,
and woodwind instruments such as flutes, and clarinets), rhythm section (piano, bass, guitars,
drums and minor percussion), vocal section (choir and main vocalist) and an optional string
section (violins, violas and cellos). They perform musical genres such as salsa, merengue,
bachata and cumbia, adapted to this format.
• The picós refer to the sound systems that take on large dimensions during the carnival and
perform electronic music genres or fusions, such as champeta and dance-hall. It should be
noted that it is also called picó to any sound system that seeks to amplify any type of music,
including international electronic or mainstream music.
All this music always sounds during the four days of carnival in every corner of the city. However,
carnival music actually sounds long before those days. Before Christmas, Barranquilleros6 can feel the
carnival not only because the comparsas (carnival groups or troups) that have been preparing and
practicing their choreographies tirelessly in the streets, squares, parks and community halls of the city,
but also because many artists take advantage of these moments of joy to launch their songs hoping
that some of them will have a special importance in the next carnival. For this reason, many Christmas
songs are confused in Barranquilla with carnival songs.
There is precisely one song that annually reminds Barranquilleros that the Christmas period is the
starting point of carnival and it is Las cuatro fiestas [The Four Parties], composed by maestro Adolfo
Echeverría in 1960 and popularized by the voice of Nury Borrás in a 1961 recording (Cuadros 2021;
Maldonado Ortega 2018). This song speaks of the dates that, without exception, all those born in
Barranquilla should celebrate: El Día de las Velitas [Day of the little Candles] (January 7/January 8),
Christmas, New Year's Day and, finally, the carnival festivities (Rincón Vanegas 2020). In its own way,
this song implies that music is a vehicle that traces a cyclical life path of what is truly important to the

Carnival music has, in turn, many spaces for development and dissemination during these festivities.
On the one hand, there are the physical spaces such as parades, verbenas and k-zs (casetas)7, discos,
clubs, street parties, concerts, in private homes and practically any commercial establishment, be it a
shopping mall, a large supermarket or a grocery store (Bassi & Solano 1999, 2017; Rey Sinning 1992;
Wade 2000). It is noteworthy that on the third day of carnival and since 1969, the concentration of the
public attending this great event is focused on the Festival of Orchestras (Rey Sinning 1992). Individual
artists and groups of different musical genres and nationalities compete from the afternoon of that day
until the early morning of the following day to win the Congo de Oro, the highest award given by the
organizers to the best exponents of regional music during the carnival. To give their verdict, the jurors
take into account criteria such as tuning and musical coupling, orchestration and creativity, originality,
technique and instrumental execution, vocal performance and interpretation, staging, stage
management and audience acceptance (Carnaval de Barranquilla S.A.S).
On the other hand, there is the radio, which helped and still help to shape what it is understood today

we see that artists and groups try to promote their musical projects not only on the radio, but in digital
spaces like Youtube or Spotify. Their goal is always the same: to position their song as "The Carnival
song", appropriate and strongly requested by the carnival public. This represents large sums of money,
not only because of the royalties for reproduction that musicians accumulate through these new
portals and applications thanks to the number of clicks made by fans of their songs, but for their
possible participation in private or public concerts during the carnival, such as the Festival of
Orchestras, which in turn means a factor of prestige.

2.1. Narratives, discourses, and representations of the music of Barranquilla Carnival
In "The Content of the Form: Narrative, Discourse and Historical Representation" (1990), Hayden
White developed the concepts of narrative, discourse and representation as fundamental tools for
understanding how historical knowledge is constructed and communicated through historical writing,
both in academia and in general culture. These concepts highlight the idea that there are no objective
and neutral narratives, but that all forms of knowledge are cultural constructions, which in turn allow
us to interpret and understand the world around us, help us to be critical and offer other different
visions of reality.
White was a historian and theorist of history and that why the text explores representations in the
field of history, but also expands it to other types of products such as visual ones, in particular it
exposes photography as a type of representation. Consequently, and due to the broad nature of his
ideas, the other two concepts addressed by him in the book (narratives and discourses) are applicable
to the field of history as well as other humanities as they can all focus on the analysis and interpretation
of the ways in which knowledge is constructed and communicated. In the same way these concepts
can be transferred to the field of ethnomusicology since all three concepts can analyze and understand
music and musical practices in specific cultural contexts, with actors who (i) create narratives through
experiences, stories, beliefs, values, collective histories and meanings associated with music and its
function in society; (ii) embody representations related to their preferences, including musical or extra-
musical aspects such as dress, dance, scenography and iconography associated with music empathize,
and (iii) ignore or reject discourses related, for example, to identity and power dynamics.
Because the book was published in the 1980s, subsequent generations of historians, theorists and
scholars interested in the theory of history, the philosophy of history and cultural studies have been
influenced, debating and discussing its impact on the academy to this day.
2.1.1. Carnival narratives
Narrative refers to the way stories are told or events are recounted. They follow a structure, that is,
they have an introduction, a development and a conclusion. They are literary or rhetorical
constructions, so they can be both fictional and non-fictional, and reflect the interpretations and

perspectives that an individual - who may be a historian or, in the present case, the different voices
that make up a comparsa - selects and organizes in a coherent and meaningful way. In this way,
narratives can be based on real facts, personal experiences, news published in the media, but also on
myths, legends, local traditions or simply on the creativity and imagination of the narrator.
In this way it can be said that the narratives of the Carnival of Barranquilla include all the elements
that are told about the carnival could include the history of the carnival, its origins, its evolution over
time, the work to turn the event into a local, national and world heritage, and the stories of the
different traditions and characters that participate in the event. The carnival has many narratives that
have been accumulated and complemented thanks to the work of different actors such as historians,
cultural managers, the media, carnival organizers, carnival makers (artists who create and participate
in the parades and other activities of the event), the public, sponsors and academics. In this way they
can be captured in journalistic notes, interviews, written memoirs, fiction books about the carnival,
descriptions of the dances and costumes, legends and myths about the origin of the costumes.
A narrative about the music of the Barranquilla Carnival could be a chronicle about the adaptation in
tropical orchestra format of a traditional Colombian cumbia played in a private club during the carnival
in the 1940's. This narrative would follow a traditional narrative structure, presenting a chronological
and coherent sequence of events with a beginning, a development and an end. The narrative could be
told by one of the musicians of the group 20 years later and could focus on the difficulties the members
had in choosing the song and adapting it, on the decisions they made about the instrumentation and
arrangements, the experiences they had during the performance, and it is possible that many things
did not happen as they did, but that is how he remembered it and that is how this narrative continued
to be replicated for many years. Another possibility could be that this chronicle was told by a journalist
from a local media the day after the presentation from his perspective as a member of the audience
and could focus on his emotions during the concert, the reception of this proposal and the little
publicity given to the event.
2.1.2. Carnival discourses
White considers discourses to be the systems of rules and conventions that shape the way in which
historical narratives are produced and interpreted. Language, narrative structures, theories and
ideologies construct discourses that serve to induce the way in which certain stories are told, but also
esteem and promote the stories that are told and those that are ignored. White explains that they are
cultural products that are subject to change and transformation over time.
Thus, Barranquilla Carnival discourses present specific approaches that shape the way Carnival events
are told. These approaches are situated under a wide range of perspectives and opinions that could
include social, political or economic issues, for example, on power, in its class or gender variants,

representations are rather subjective and selective, and may convey certain meanings and
interpretations while omitting others.

published by tourist agencies, the facade and contents of the carnival museum supported by the

spaces. In this sense, the representations can also come from political leaders, cultural figures, carnival

the band in order not to be out of tune with the environment and its audience. It could also be the
photos taken by the journalist that perhaps emphasize not only the orchestra but the context in which
it is located (private social club, elegant, aimed at local elites, without much racial or socioeconomic
diversity). A final example could be a video of a current band performing a cover version of the same
song emulating the style and manner in which it might have been performed in the last century or
parodying it by showing the band members as people who are out of tune with their surroundings.
2.2. Narratives of Carnival from the perspective of globalization
The city of Barranquilla and its carnival have changed over the years due to various reasons that impact
the social, political and economic order. In the same way, the stories of its participants (the narratives)
have changed, and with it, the different discourses and representations. In other words, in order to
identify the interests of the carnival actors through their discourses and the expression through their
representations, it is worthwhile to understand the different narratives about the carnival that have
been documented and that can be related to the different periods of the city of Barranquilla, with
respect to its carnival. This turns out to be quite an arduous task due to the amount of existing
information. For this purpose, it was necessary to resort to Arjun Appadurai's studies on globalization,
particularly those associated with the dimensions of globalization (Appadurai 1996; See Appendix E).
This made it possible to classify the narratives taking into account three historical periods.
To begin with, Barranquilla is not a city that originated during the colony, although it received much
influence from it because it is part of a country that was colonized. It was proclaimed a village on April
7, 1813 by the governor of the Free State of Cartagena, Manuel Rodríguez Torices, before obtaining
the title of city in 1857 (Alcaldía de Barranquilla 2020; Amaya 2023). It is important to know this,
because the first narratives of Carnival are those that are circumscribed from the nineteenth century,
when the city began to form, this being a first period. The second period we will take it from the 30's
of the 20th century when the country began to interact with other countries due to new technological
advances such as the radio and television. The last period begins with the new century in which we live
and coincides with the declaration of the Carnival of Barranquilla as national and world heritage.

2.2.1. Carnival narratives, established from the formation period
This period is characterized as the starting point of Barranquilla as a new city that welcomed people
from other parts of the country and other nations, specially from Europe, Middle East, and Asia. At this
time the city welcomed elements, entrepreneurships, and traditions of people from other origins,
including, for example, artifacts such as musical instruments and the creation of businesses. The
foreign must have molded or merged with the local society as a result of the colonial era, whose
population was composed of a mixture of Spaniards, Africans and indigenous people (Polifroni Lobo
2022). It is at this time that the Colombian peso was created as the country's currency (Albarracín
Restrepo 2023). Communication was mainly through pamphlets and newspapers, and it was a time
when values such as freedom and sovereignty flourished, typical of a young nation, but also others
such as multiculturalism due to the reception of migrants.
2.2.1.1. Early narratives of the carnival: origin and first years of carnival in the city
The oldest historical accounts date from the second half of the 19th century (Buelvas 2006). They
describe the first parades, the floats and the costumes of participants of the event, and present the
figure of Rey Momo [King Momo], a fictitious character shared by many Latin American carnivals, who
is generally a man who has contributed significantly to the construction of the Carnival and who is

participants. These stories are mainly found in old newspapers.
It is important to say that from the second period (Formation) historians begin to inquire about what
happened at this time and it is when the creation of the carnival is narrated in more detail going back
to the eighteenth century when in Cartagena, a colonial city neighboring Barranquilla, the "Cabildos
de Cartagena" were held. Over there the enslaved population of African origin and their descendants
parodied the Hispanic hierarchical institutions and the abolition of privileges in celebrations that lasted
all day until dawn and were annoying and frowned upon by the church and government authorities.
They explain that the Carnival of Barranquilla was actually a celebration transferred from Cartagena.
According to them the carnival shows how slaves took control and their role in a society that oppressed
them. This narrative can be seen in books and academic articles written by González Henríquez (2006),
Miranda Freitas (2013) and Abello Vives & Macías (2021). Other researchers of this period go even
further back and review the origin of Carnival from ancient Greek and Roman times, through the
Saturnalian festivities or simply “Saturnalia” (Covo Torres 1990; Friedemann 1985; Orozco Cantillo &
Soto Mazenett 1997; Soto Mazenett 1999). The above are complemented by the ideas of the classical
theory of carnival approached by Bakhtin (Bakhtin 1987; Lachmann 1988-89) who sees in carnival a
temporal space in which hierarchies are inverted, since it breaks with all the structures, conventions
and institutions of society, through rites and symbols that highlight corporeality, its material excesses,
the grotesque (seen as the superimposition of opposing elements such as the serious and the comic,
or the sacred and the profane), polyphony as opposed to a single dominant voice, and laughter,
understood not only as entertainment, but as a form of liberation. They focus mainly on explaining
how the party of today is related to the rites of the past.
2.2.2. Carnival narratives, established from the institutionalization period
This period begins with the arrival of the phonograph and radio in Colombia and the popularization of
orchestras in big band format in Barranquilla (Hernández Salgar2014; Rey Sinning 1992). During the
early years of this time Barranquilla experienced an economic boom as a result of an accelerated
industrialization that was largely developed by the national and foreign migrants of the first period and
that seduced people such as peasants and fishermen of the region in search of job opportunities. These
then became the salaried workers of the local Barranquilla companies. A process of rapid urbanization
was evidenced, but with an unequal development that resulted in the gentrification of the elites and
the exclusion of the less favored (Rey Sinning 1996). The second part of the 20th century saw the
beginning of the current Colombian armed conflict that brought with it the forced displacement of
thousands of people from rural areas to large cities such as Barranquilla and also established the

2.2.2.1. Narratives of carnival from the folklore studies

that today are associated with the Carnival of Barranquilla and that are part of the collective
imagination. This category includes not only journalistic articles, but also texts written by folklore
professionals that describe the origin of each of the dances, costumes and events. Although they are
descriptive in nature, they always seek to highlight those elements that separate the Carnival of
Barranquilla from other carnivals, highlighting and idealize those elements that are not repeated
elsewhere, unlike the early narratives of the carnival that always seek to review the origin of the
carnival through the common elements it has with other carnivals. Cultural narratives in folklore can
include various types of expressions, such as tales, legends, myths, songs, proverbs, sayings,
anecdotes, oral histories and any other form of storytelling that is important to the carnival. They are
mainly narrated by folklorists, libraries, archives and museums, but also by government entities.

2.2.2.2. Carnival narratives from heritage studies
It is based on the narratives of carnival from the folklore studies; however, it focuses on the heritage

international organizations that have an interest in preserving such manifestations and promoting

Its quest to give meaning to heritage allows giving voice to many people who can enrich the reasons

the sustainability of these manifestations. The Barranquilla Carnival is approached from this narrative

2.2.2.3. Institutional carnival narratives
Like carnival narratives from heritage studies, institutional carnival narratives are also supported by
narratives of carnival from the folklore studies. It is that specific information provided by governmental
institutions, tourist assistance or media to the general public that attends, participates and is
interested in what happens in the carnival. It is tourist information, promotion of events, calls for
competitions, last minute announcements, safety warnings, notices for the promotion of civility and
generally positive messages about what it means to live the carnival and the city of Barranquilla.
They are generally narratives that are promoted by public institutions or tourist agencies, by the
entities in charge of security and are disseminated through pamphlets, press releases, mass media
such as television and radio, the pages and social networks of the Mayor's Office of Barranquilla,
Colombia or the organizers of the events, billboards and banners. An example of these narratives can
be seen in the requirements to participate in the Orchestras Festival contest, in the statistical data of
the economic gains obtained during the carnival, mobility information or in the tourist brochures that
show historical facts or memories of the carnival that are important for the city or activities organized
by the mayor's office with information of other events not related to the carnival.

2.2.2.4. Carnival narratives from Academia
They are narratives of a critical nature, made by scholars and experts in various disciplines such as
anthropology, sociology, history, cultural studies, literature and ethnography, economics, among
others. Through analysis, they seek to understand the carnival from deeper and contextualized
perspectives, exploring its cultural, historical, social, political, economic and symbolic meaning. They

2.2.3. Carnival narratives, established from the integration period
This period is related to the declaration of Declaration of the Carnival as a national heritage site in 2001
and as a world heritage site in 2003. This created the conditions for the development of an ever-

networks such as MySpace (2003), Facebook (2004), Youtube (2005), Whatsapp (2009), Instagram
2010 and TikTok (2016), which meant the birth of virtual communities that in turn began to use
smartphones, tablets and other new technological equipment that accelerated information
management and network interaction through apps and platforms with streaming content. These
facilities forced the tightening of copyrights, however, the rapid crossing of opinions also allowed the
presence of ideas such as those favoring the representation of minorities and gender equity.

2.3. Carnival representations from the perspective of globalization
The Carnival of Barranquilla, throughout its history has represented its different cultural manifestations
in different ways taking into account the different contexts that were presented in each era. The
intertwining of cultural influences, as well as those that over time began to arrive from other parts of
the world added to the result of the representations in each period. Although the scope of the Carnival
at this time is international, making its current representations recognized, we see today how there is
still a process, integrating more elements and creating new forms.
2.3.1. Carnival representations, established from the formation period
In this period we can see how some of the most important traditions of carnival begin to be created
and adopt the form we know today. For example, some of the costumes and dances such as El Torito
date back to approximately 1880, as well as some of the central characters of the carnival, such as Rey
Momo and the Carnival Queen (Rey Sinning 1992). Manifestations that today are tradition, such as the
"Reading of El Bando", with which the carnival is initiated through "decrees", was created in 1876, or
La Batalla de Flores [The Battle of Flowers], the most popular parade of the carnival, were created
before 1930 (Ortega 2023; Pacheco Toscano 2020). Old photos from this period show how even many

2.3.2. Carnival representations, established from the institucionalization period
In this period all the previous traditions (costumes, activities, dances) are already more established in
the event and many new ones appear. Some of the new activities that are adopted are El Festival de
Orquestas [The festival of Orchestras] in 1969, La Gran Parada [The Great Parade] in 1967 and La

commerce and had strong ties with Europe and the United States (Oliveros 2006). For this reason, it is
understandable to see how foreign big band orchestras become very popular during carnivals, bringing
foreign musical genres to Barranquilla. Television has also played an important role in the generation
of representations, for example, in the creation of costumes inspired by highly notorious characters in
this media, such as local, national and international entertainment personalities and politicians.
Similarly, the carnival began to be presented on local channels with national coverage (Montes 2017).
2.3.3. Carnival representations, established from the integration period
While the carnival was declared national and international heritage, the development of technology
had a strong impact on the Carnival representations. Carnival ceased to be presented only in media
such as television, to be shown through the internet and social networks. At the same time, viral
phenomena of digital platforms began to make their presence felt by integrating themselves into the
Carnival representations. We are talking about popular music such as those using electronic sounds,
but also about social media influencers (Caracol 2019; Palacio 2018). Critical voices against companies
and brands began to be increasingly greater due to the prominence that these were having in front of
the artistic manifestations proper to the carnival (Tubarco 2023; Oyaga de Luque 2023).

2.4. Authenticity as discourse
For this section, it was important to examine the writings of Salge Ferro (2020) who identifies three
major strands of the discourse on authenticity. To arrive at this, he resorts to the paradox of Theseus
who wonders if when all the parts are replaced, an object remains the same. Salge Ferro explains that
the first answer to this question is answered "from a conception tied to the Platonic tradition, every
change in the materiality of the ship would imply a degradation of its being" (Salge Ferro 2020 : 52), in
other words, keeping the ship without any alteration keeps it "authentic". On the other hand, he
explains that in the process of replacing materials, methods can be used that allow its sustainability
over time and this process of innovation is what should be considered "authentic". The last aspect
stems from the subject and not from the object, i.e. authenticity is tied to the type of experience the
subject has had when considering an element, context or situation as novel. This position, taken up by
the author of heritage studies, is generated by the understanding that the relationship between an
object and a subject can be "cold" (which requires a certificate, declaration, prior supporting act) or
"warm" (which generates a strong bond in the subject without the need for a previous document or
act to endorse it).
2.4.1. Essentialist authenticity
This aspect, also called real or objective authenticity, was explored by Kim, Oh, Lee & Lee (2018),
Wesener (2017) and Wang (1999) considers authentic what can be associated with the adjectives
genuine, real, true, uncompromising or absolute. This means that there is a strong binarism with what
it considers the opposite, i.e. what is false, fraudulent, simulated, copy. The value of the object
(element, context or situation) lies in its origin and the fact that it belongs to someone.
This concept is closely linked to Romantic Nationalism, which tries to rescue and enhance those mores
that are considered authentic, unique, pure, and even vernacular (Abrahams 1993). Johann Gottfried
Herder was the founder of this movement, and he described a set of tenets, for example, the existence
of different historical ages in nations, which forms an independent cultural entity, whose parts are
integrally related to form an organic whole and the necessity of every nationality to develop in accord

-- 22 of 100 --

22
with its own innate abilities, geographical environment, and innate cultural elements (Wilson 1973).
In the Carnival of Barranquilla this is observed in the iron stance of keeping certain traditions intact,
this includes costumes, dances and carnival rites, such as always celebrating the end of the carnival
with the death of a fictitious character called Joselito Carnaval.
2.4.2. Constructivist authenticity
The second variant refers to understanding authenticity as a developing element that is valued. Kim,
Oh, Lee & Lee (2018), Wesener (2017) and Wang (1999) conceive this type of authenticity as a heritage
under construction that watches over a social process of constant creation and recreation. In this
respect, authenticity is measured in the beliefs, values and preferences that people negotiate and
project onto themselves. For this reason, this view understands that authenticity is not static and may
change over time.
This type of authenticity solves the problem of seeing certain elements, contexts or situations that are
valued as simple photographs that must remain intact over time, which meant a broader vision of
heritage as it was previously understood. In this way, national or international organizations were able
to review their position on what should be studied, protected and celebrated, and that perhaps before
was not taken care of in the best way despite its non-fluid value. Thus, rituals and ceremonies
performed by individuals or collectives that are adjusted over the years for various reasons can be
elements seen as authentic and not only the enclosures where such events take place that generally
remain unchanged. This position strongly benefits the performative processes created from
spontaneity and innovation over time. In the Carnival of Barranquilla this position can be observed
when adaptations are made to the existing traditions, for example, the floats that used to be rather
small and decorated with flowers, are now monumental and full of carnival motifs.
2.4.3. Experiental authenticity
The third variant, also called existential authentictity (Wesener 2017; Kim, Oh, Lee & Lee 2018; Yi, Lin,
Jin & Luo 2017), occurs when relating to a manifestation and receiving an authentic experience with
elements, contexts or situations that are not necessarily authentic for other people. This depends on
the subject's perception, subjective and intrapersonal or as something shared, communal and
interpersonal, particularly in the context of tourism, cultural encounters, and consumption. It focuses
on the extent to which an individual's experience of a place, event, or product aligns with their
expectations, desires, and perceptions of what is considered authentic.

This also indicates that for there to be a type of authentic experience it is important to know the
current context of the individual or collective that considers the elements, contexts, or situations as
unique. Thus, a person who has had many experiences similar to what he or she considers authentic
may do so based on comparisons. In the Carnival of Barranquilla this can be seen in the photos that
people post on social networks such as Instagram during a carnival event in which they make explicit
that this period was particularly special or unique in their life.
3. Methodology
Having explained what the narratives, representations and discourses are, understanding the
development of the first two terms (narratives and representation) taking into account a globalization
approach in the carnival, and emphasizing the need to reflect and identify the different narratives and
representations about Carnival due to the interrelationship that these may have with any discourse,
especially those that are of interest for this work, which are the discourses of authenticity, it is time to
introduce our case study, the comparsa “La Puntica No Má”, with its own narratives and
representations. However, it is important to first explain the methodology used to establish contact

The proposed methodology involves first clarifying the type and methodological approach that has
been chosen, then how the data will be obtained to answer these questions, the schedule of activities
carried out and finally an explanation of how the data will be analyzed and interpreted.
3.1. Type and methodological approach
The methodological design was a mixed case study that combined both qualitative and quantitative
elements. Due to the nature of the proposed project, the methodology chosen follows the lines of
"participant observation". It can be defined as "one of the several methods that fit into the general
category of qualitative research" and consists of "structured and semi-structured interviewing, pure
observation and the collection and analysis of text" (DeWalt & DeWalt 2011: 2). According to DeWalt
& DeWalt, the importance of this methodology is mainly because:
i) it allows for better quality of the data obtained, since much of the information that exists is
tacit and must be gained through empirical processes;
ii) it allows a better quality in the interpretation of data, especially with those related to non-
verbal communication, but also considering the closeness one has with the people with whom
one interacts and who are the source of data, so that one, as a researcher, can anticipate
reactions and responses of different natures;

-- 24 of 100 --

24
iii) it consolidates personal relationships in the case of long-term research, which can provide
greater depth and detail of data and be important in the event of wanting to continue with
research in the future; and
iv) it encourages the formulation of new research questions.
This methodology allows to make more sense of the context due to the level of commitment of the
researcher, who participates directly in the research events, and because of the interaction he/she has
with the subjects who also participate in them. In this sense, the researcher presents himself/herself
as a participant and at the same time as an observer. This does not mean that the methodology focused
solely on qualitative data, since part of the information analyzed was taken from data that were
quantified through surveys and other mechanisms.
To carry out a participant observation meant for the researcher to apply to be part of the group during
the 2023 edition of the carnival. Permission was granted as long as the researcher went through all the
regulatory process that the group has in order to receive membership. For this reason it was necessary
to present an original design of a costume according to the theme chosen by the group for the year
2023, make a review of the design concept and explain how a character would be developed based on
the costume and design, i.e. the performative element. In the call for entries they were emphatic that
the proposal had to be in line with the comparsa's standards. The slogan of the comparsa of the 2023
edition was "Flower me the seed", a somewhat ambiguous phrase that makes clear sexual references,
and its theme pointed to the collective creation of a garden with all its elements: plants, birds, stones,
insects, fungi, etc. Once the proposal (see in Appendix F) was approved, the next step was the payment
of money to secure a place in the 2023 edition.
Another element to consider is that, in this case, the researcher was born and raised in the city of
Barranquilla. This assumes that the researcher has prior experience and knowledge of the city which
could be an advantage in having a deeper understanding of the culture, history and social dynamics of
the community. There could also be concerns about unconscious biases due to ingrained
preconceptions about the city, the carnival, and the comparsa, which is their case study. In turn, the
researcher is perceived as part of the community or not depending on his socioeconomic reality and
previous relationships with community members.
Finally, there were ethical considerations for the research, such as informed consent, confidentiality,
respect for the participants and the handling of sensitive data.

After presenting the comparsa La Puntica No Má' in the next chapter, it is time to show what was
found given the objectives of the project and in order to generate a data analysis and its subsequent
interpretation, it was necessary to address the research questions, which are “How can discourses on
authenticity be identified through the music listened to and enjoyed by the members of a carnival
comparsa?” and “What discourses on authenticity about the Carnival of Barranquilla can currently
be recognized in the music listened to and enjoyed by the members of the same collective costume
troupe, such as La Puntica no má’?” In reviewing the research questions, we note that there are certain

In order to answer the first question it is important to 1) review the relationship between La Puntica

4. Case of study: La Puntica
No Má'
La Puntica No Má', or simply "La Puntica", is a collective costume
comparsa of Barranquilla Carnival10. For the collective costume
groups, the creation of the costumes and their interpretation during
the carnival are the most important elements. Other collective costume comparsas, which are still
active today, are Disfrázate Como Quieras, whose former members of La Puntica they call comparsa
madre [mother comparsa], since they were members of the latter who created La Puntica No Má';
there are also La Nave de l@s Loc@s and El Desacato, comparsas that broke away from La Puntica No
Má', so we could say that they are “daughter” comparsas - or granddaughter comparsas, depending
on who name it (Pinilla Acevedo 2023).
La Puntica No Má' defines itself on its website as "a collective performance, a meeting space within
the Carnival of Barranquilla. A joyful and spiritual experience that encourages you to explore your inner
self and live art as a creative engine. Experiencing it leaves you transformed and inspired" (La Puntica
No Má’ 2023). In the case of La Puntica No Má', each year there is a theme assigned by the organizers,

This collective costume troupe was born in 1999 and was an idea materialized by Daniel Angulo, Ingrid
Jamis and plastic artist Flavia Rosales as a fresh and alternative way to connect tradition with
contemporary art aesthetics. Since its creation, emphasis has been placed on the role of drum music
in the meetings and parties that took place in the house of Daniel Angulo, who was the first director of
the comparsa, and his wife Ingrid Jamis, to determine the way in which the group would express itself
during the carnivals. In 2004 Flavia Rosales and Francisco González took over the direction. Today, the
comparsa has a board of directors that is composed of an artistic direction, which is in charge of
everything related to artistic curatorship, makeup, music, decoration of events, etc., and an
administrative direction that is in charge of marketing, networks, accounting, etc. This board of
directors is accompanied by an assembly, which is made up solely of the founders of the comparsa,
with whom they meet to make the important decisions of the collective. La Puntica No Ma' has
expanded its mission objectives as it not only seeks to have an impact with its proposal during its
participation within the Carnival spaces, but also carries out events such as parties outside the Carnival
period and creates cultural projects related to themes such as Carnival and the arts, such as the
recording of a musical album (Carvajal 2023; La Puntica No Ma' 2023).

La Puntica No Má' is characterized by its double meaning, its sexual humor and its aesthetic proposals
that have always stood out since its foundation for being considered daring, ingenious, irreverent and
uncomfortable (Angulo 2023). Their website explains that La Puntica No Má' is "the upside-down world
where all norms and rules are subverted to give way to celebration and excessive ingestion, hierarchical
suppression, laughter, play, inversions, degradations and ambivalence" (La Puntica No Má' 2023). In a
certain way it represents the act of embracing carnival according to the classical interpretation of
carnival. The very name of the comparsa, in English "just the tip", is a play on words as it has clear
erotic/sexual references. La Puntica No Má’ seeks to vindicate the history that has been built through
power structures that come from colonial times and that have molded society in such a way that favors
some and oppresses others. The comparsa's website recalls that Caribbean ancestors lived through
"colonial and cultural domination and the various threats against the expression of identity to which
they were subjected. This search for identity is undertaken mainly and vehemently from the artistic
field" (La Puntica No Má' 2023).
There is a vocabulary specific to the comparsa. For example, the member or everything related to the
comparsa is called punteros. New members of the comparsa are called vírgenes [virgins]. There are
also certain sayings, litanies and iconic places for this group. The verbena (carnival night party) of La
Puntica on Carnival Saturday night has become a very referenced event. The truth is that year after
year the comparsa seems to remind us that it is constantly creating and inventing its own rites,
coinciding with its saying that dates back to the early years "tradition in evolution". These values must
be followed by the members of the comparsa especially by the captains of the comparsa who are
chosen each year by the council.
To be a member of La Puntica is important to fill out a form, in which you must enter your personal and
contact information, if you know someone in the comparsa and submit the proposed character
(costume + performance) according to the theme of the following year. Additionally, a fee must be paid,
which varies according to nationality and years, which for nationals costed $472,500 (cost in Colombian
pesos, the local currency) in 2023, a rather high amount for many Colombians, considering that the
member must also build a character whose costume can cost quite a lot of money. For foreigners the
fee is higher.

La Puntica is always trying to rescue the traditional, but teleported towards the future... Towards a

for those not fluent in the local language. Broadly speaking, it can be said that both the residence and
place of birth of the members is mostly Colombia. However, there is a considerable component of
foreign population or members living abroad, which is around 20%. The age range with the highest
participation in the comparsa is between 30 and 39 years of age, almost 50% of the total. In terms of
gender, the surveys gave fairly balanced results if we consider only the categories of male and female,
however, there was one person who presented himself as non-binary. When people were asked about
their sexual orientation we see that approximately 58 percent of them define themselves as
heterosexual, which indicates that the rest could fall into the "LGBT+" category: 13 people presented
themselves as homosexual, 3 people as bisexual and 1 person preferred not to answer. In terms of
socioeconomic strata, we see that the comparsa participants live mainly in households considered

than 5 years.

music format. When members were asked "With which of these carnival music formats do you feel

how they have become part of its identity. The representations allow the comparsa (both the
organization and its members) to transfer in an artistic way those values and ideologies that the
narratives fail to convey by simply communicating them. That is, those elements that are highlighted
both in the narratives and in the representations. If we ask ourselves, what are these values and
ideologies? The answer would be in the discourses.
Remembering what White said, discourses are systems of rules and conventions that shape the way in
which historical narratives are produced and interpreted and where the actor of the discourse
highlights certain elements over others, showing a clear position on them in order to influence the
position of others regarding a specific topic and that can be proven by observing a multiplication of
such discourse through new narratives, a propagation that could also take the form of representations.
This opens the door to think that, although the representations do not necessarily explain their ideas
explicitly with words, they can do so by highlighting other elements, which can be musical or extra-
musical. In other words, in order to identify which are the discourses in cumbia and champeta, musics
that play an important role in the comparsa, it would be necessary to make a triangulation between
those elements that are most highlighted in the narratives and at the same time that stand out in the
representations of these musical genres in La Puntica No Má'.
The result of this analysis made it possible to identify three types of discourses, based on the
approaches that comparsa members may have towards certain musical genres or formats. These
discourses coincide with the discourses of authenticity identified by Salge (2020), but unlike him this
analysis did not focus on the public and their publications on social networks from the field of image,
but rather, on the carnival makers themselves, in this case taking as an example the comparsa of La
Puntica No Má', in the field of music.
5.1. First discourse: Cumbia de Chorrera, as a source of authentic ancestry
The narratives of La Puntica No Má' show cumbia, specifically the Chorrera variant, and the millo
format, with its drums, maracas and flutes, as a foundational and fundamental milestone for the
comparsa. On the one hand, we see how Daniel Angulo, co-founder of the group, advocates the
nostalgia and ancestry of cumbia for the conception of La Puntica. Interviews with members show how
they appreciate the presence of cumbia, describing it as "the original" or "the most traditional". An
American member, approximately 38 years old, who said he has participated in the comparsa in many
editions, said that La Puntica No Má' is only for those who are interested in cumbia and traditional
music. In the same way, this rhythm has been from the beginning of the comparsa a resource for

-- 45 of 100 --

45
experimentation and the resulting developments are well received by the leaders and other members
under the slogan "Tradition in evolution". A 37-year-old member of the comparsa from Barranquilla
describes these new proposals as a mix of more globalized music such as funk and electronic music
with cumbia.
On the other hand, cumbia is shown as a genre they call "mother", referring to the fact that cumbia is
the origin of other musical genres such as bullerengue or mapalé. For example, Georghe Pinilla, in one
part of the interview, he expresses “cumbia, obviously mother cumbia, [that] is the key to our carnival."
Finally, there is the fact that cumbia is highlighted for its tri-ethnic origins: African, European and Native
American. This is especially evident when they use the word "raizal" to define cumbia and millo,
referring to "something that has different roots or origins" and not to "a person from the islands of San
Andres and Providencia" which is how many Colombians understand this term. In the interviews with
the members of the comparsa, it can be seen how they also attribute certain values, such as "strength"
or "tastiness" to the African contribution.
In the same way, the performances of La Puntica No Má' show how cumbia and the millo format stand
out within the musical universe. Cumbia de Chorrera is seen as the variant that embodies the more
traditional side of the comparsa and the group Los Originales de Chorrera (in English, The Originals of
Chorrera) is present in many events organized by La Puntica No Má'. The music of Los Originales de
Chorrera has dialogued with musicians associated with the comparsa such as Andy Uproot, creating
musical proposals that unfold new horizons for the same musical genre. Daniel Angulo, seeing him not
as co-founder of the group but rather as a creative musician, presents a repertoire where he fuses
electronic resonances with millo and cumbia sounds and whose texts are related to the comparsa and
the carnival. This repertoire can be seen in his SoundCloud21 profile where he has versions of famous
cumbias like "El Pescador", but also cumbias composed by him like "La Pollera Arrebata'". What is
interesting is that these songs are listed in a playlist that he titles "Kumbias [sic] del Olvido" [Cumbias
del olvido], where there are not only cumbias, but also other songs with other musical genres such as
"La Quinceañera", which is not a cumbia but rather a son de garabato but that many could consider
that it fits within the umbrella of cumbia, for being a "son" of this musical genre. The lyrics of the songs
in this playlist casually highlight the separate instruments and their sounds are associated with certain
emotions as when in "La Pollera Arrebata" it says that "el olvido que hay en la gaita se posa en tu
mirada" [the oblivion in the gaita rests on your gaze], referring to the melancholy or sadness that the
look of the other person has, and that could be related to the indigenous contribution of the flutes to
the musical genre of cumbia and to the traditional musical format.

In the texts of the songs in this playlist, words such as "past", “ancestral” and "ritual" can be heard to
define cumbia, and in the case of "La Quinceañera" it presents the very origins of the comparsa,
associating these sonorities over and over again with the most autochthonous, the very first and the
purest. However, as Juan Sebastián Ochoa (2019) points out, there are no studies or historical and
musical analyses that provide evidence that these types of ensembles perform music that represents
the most traditional, pioneering or "ancestral" of cumbia. Moreover, it is not clear to determine the
origin of the term "cumbia" because in the middle of the twentieth century, any musical genre of
Caribbean coastal origin was stated as cumbia (J.S. Ochoa 2019). J.S. Ochoa states:
“Cumbia", like any other concept, is a social construction. In this sense, it is not an ontological
entity to be discovered, but rather a human creation that is in permanent transformation and
negotiation (2016 : 2).
The above has made music researchers reevaluate the way this musical genre has been treated with
all kinds of honors without the historical-musical rigor it deserves, and with it, lay the groundwork for
future critical analysis of this rhythm or concept, as J.S. Ochoa (2016) calls it. Among them is a series
of four premises about cumbia elaborated by Federico Ochoa that is:
1. Cumbia is recognized as one of the most important musical practices in Latin America.
2. Colombia is the country of origin of this expression, which later spread, changed and
adapted in different countries of the continent.
3. Among musicians, it is recognized that the conjunto de caña de millo is the original format
of this genre in Colombia.
4. Its epicenter is Barranquilla and its surroundings. (F. Ochoa 2019: 69)
From these premises has been developed precisely one of the imaginaries that we will deal with later,
which is that there is a "primordial" cumbia that has been changing and adapting in different contexts.
More specifically, this first imaginary relates that from a single original cumbia there has been a sort of
linear evolution. The second imaginary points out that cumbia is the mother rhythm of many other
musical genres. The third imaginary refers to its tri-ethnic origin. These three imaginaries actually
indicate that cumbia and the millo format are related to a type of essentialist authenticity discourse,
which sees this musical genre as absolute, ancestral, unique and original, and which links it to a strong
romantic nationalism with respect to Colombia but also to the Caribbean region.
5.1.1. First imaginary: The origin of everything
This first imaginary suggests that there is a single musical root of cumbia and that there has been a
clear evolution over the years. This evolution has been manifested mainly by an organological

-- 47 of 100 --

47
transformation that began with the millo flutes, coinciding with one of Federico Ochoa's (2019)
premises and continues to evolve. Juan Sebastián Ochoa (2019) explains that this imaginary has been
replicated not only by common people but also by intellectuals such as Jorge Nieves (2008), making
use of statements that seem absolute, but which have not been made an effort to review in more
depth. His critique is presented above all regarding the belief that the different formats have received
the cumbia inheritance from the previous type of group without even evaluating the degree of
influence they may have had from other musical genres to call them also cumbia.
The linear evolution according to J.S. Ochoa (2019) begins before 1900 with the millos and gaitas22
ensembles and develops until today with different formats such as wind bands and big bands (See
Appendix L). Juan Sebastián Ochoa (2019) states that this eagerness to emphasize that there is an
original format generates uncertainty, not that much because it is questioned whether or not these
instruments were really the first instruments that played cumbia, but because it seems that at all costs
the most important thing is that these instruments are easily exoticized (and fetishized23), and that is a
second criticism that is made to this imaginary.
The exoticization is given as a way to emphasize again and again that this genre represents a specific
society. A genre that was born from the exchange with many ethnic groups but that especially received
in cumbia, elements that could not be present in any other society since "the ancestral, the mythical
and its timeless past" that are represented in the millos (and in the gaitas) is the pure expression of the
indigenous peoples that were here since pre-colonial times. The interesting thing here is that these
peoples did not migrate for historical reasons as the Spaniards or the enslaved blacks from Africa might
have done. So they are instruments of a community that can be marked on a map and that today is
called the Colombian Caribbean, which, despite being part of a sovereign state that is Colombia, is
presented as a region with great autonomy in the field of culture.

(1983/1991), communities are imagined and to make this claim he considers the different political
constituencies he investigated, such as countries, dynasties and people who feel part of a specific
religion. Anderson argues that the nation is an imagined political community, which is limited, because

nation are essentially identical. In this way the instruments are reified in an exotic way to reaffirm
imagined communities: the Colombian Caribbean at first and the Colombian nation later on.
A third criticism of this imaginary is given by the simple concept of evolution, which of course is linked
to the Darwinian conception of biological evolution. This theory brought about many debates, mainly
because of the omission of the word genes, but also because of the non-use of the term "unidirectional
progress". Using these terms in a cultural field such as the one we present in this case may not conform
to the reality of the way in which complexity manifests itself in cultural processes, which is not at all
unidirectional. Maintaining the term "progression" meant being the target of much criticism because
it allowed the adoption and legitimization of ideologies of Western superiority that justified the
oppression of the weak by the powerful as part of a system that gives survival to those who can adapt
better (Hofstadter 1955; Laland, et al. 2014; Stocking 1982). Thus, to think that a wind band is more
evolved than a traditional format and that a big-band is more advanced than a wind band because of
a "natural development" is problematic, especially if one takes into account that the instruments that
represent the "primordial" ensemble are indigenous flutes, while the formats seen below are
composed of instruments more associated with Western organology.
As mentioned above, in the case of La Puntica No Má', the cumbia performed is cumbia de Chorrera.

5.1.2. Second imaginary: Mother of musical genres
This imaginary suggests that cumbia is a musical genre that is the basis for all others that exist in the
Colombian Caribbean region. In this way, cumbia represents the strengthening of its own identity
reflected in rhythms that are associated with different geographical, economic, political and cultural
contexts of the Caribbean. Some of these genres are tambora, son de garabato and porro (See
Appendix M).
This imaginary can be seen in audio-documentaries such as 40 años de música costeña - la historia de
la cumbia colombiana [40 years of coastal music - the history of Colombian cumbia], where the authors
express:
Cumbia, as it grew stronger, became a mother rhythm from which the different manifestations
of the Coast later emerged. Thus were born bullerengue, porro, puya, merengue, fandango and
other rhythms. (Apuleyo & Forero 1967 : min. 2:00)
J.S. Ochoa (2019) explains that even though these other genres have musical coincidences in terms of
their rhythmic base and organology, it is impossible to know today whether all the other rhythms were

the most important musical practices in Latin America due to its adoption and adaptation throughout
the continent. For obvious reasons, this would make Colombian cumbia the mother genre of all
cumbias that emerged from Mexico to Argentina.

same genre called cumbia, it is necessary to talk about the notion of glocalization, which refers to the

5.1.3. Third imaginary: Manifestation of a tri-ethnic racial mixture
The last imaginary refers to the mixed origin of cumbia. Juan Sebastián Ochoa (2019) explains that in
the twentieth century a dispute was generated about the ethnic origin of cumbia. On the one hand,
personalities such as musicians José Barros and Pacho Galán argued that the indigenous root was what
allowed the creation of the musical genre, while others such as anthropologist and physician Manuel
Zapata Olivella, folklorist Guillermo Abadía and musicians Efraín Medina and Lucho Bermúdez pointed
out that it was an imminently black musical genre. This led to ideological discussions that can be
considered racist and not supported by empirical evidence. for example, it was said that the indigenous
origin was paramount because cumbia at that time was seen as "measured, demure and wise, far from
the eroticism and freneticism associated with the black people" (J.S. Ochoa 2019 : 39-40), while the
opposite side thought that everything that could be "exoticized" should be considered as a sample of
the cultural legacy of the African diaspora in the country.
It was not until the arrival of the article "Cumbia: musical synthesis of the Colombian nation" by
folklorist, dancer and pedagogue Delia Zapata, sister of Manuel Zapata, that a new position on its origin
appeared. In the text she points out:
The proof of the origin of cumbia is linked to the integration of the American cocktail and goes back
to the roots of our tri-ethnic ancestor, whose three ingredients, already mixed in different
proportions, form the synthesis of the Colombian Nation. [...]
The proper twang of the instruments that accompany with their music, the choreography of
cumbia shows it: drums of black accent; flutes of indigenous groan, the dress and the singing reveal
the Hispanic style. [...]

-- 52 of 100 --

52
A team or choreographic ensemble in which the rhythmic euphoria of the black people, the
melancholy cadence of the Indian and the gracefulness of the Spaniard converge. It is like a musical
synthesis of our nationality (Zapata 1962 : 190-200).
Mariano Candela (2003), Ana María Tamayo (2013), Juan Sebastián Ochoa (2016) and Federico Ochoa
(2014) explain that this sentence is presented as a more political than historical solution that helps to
create an idea of cumbia that clings to positive images or emotions of each ethnic component, which
J.S. Ochoa (2019) reinterprets as: the joy of the black, the sobriety of the indigenous and the elegance
and haughtiness of the European. He also explained that this proposal represents a process of
whitening by the dominant classes, who preferred cumbia for being a dance seen as calm, measured,
demure, controlled, haughty and elegant, perfect for the symbolization of a civilized Colombia, as
opposed to other dances such as fandango, mapalé or puya seen as disorderly, erotic and wild. In other
words, for J.S. Ochoa, the black and indigenous components were a function of the European element,
thus reminding us of images of the colony where the Europeans were supported by the blacks and
Indians, as well as their resources (See Appendix N).
Today it can be said that the discussion on the origin of cumbia is over, since the adoption of Delia
Zapata's proposal is generalized in all kinds of articles in private media and official sources (Carbó 2006;
Juáregui 2023). The researcher Carlos Miñana pointed out that by being very "idealized, embalmed
and consecrated by the authority of folklorists[, the questioning of this cultural project may] hurt
popular sensitivity, that is, deny the identity, the roots, the 'proper' values of Colombian culture. It is
to be a stateless person who -in certain contexts- deserves to be lynched, or at least excluded" (1999:
36). As J.S. Ochoa (2019) indicates in this type of cases it is more difficult to deconstruct than to
construct the myth about cumbia because of the symbolic power that it carries and that prevents
contradicting cultural institutions, which are the reflection of the image of a society that perceives itself
as mestizo, and that protects them under the pretext of being necessary for the structuring and
consolidation of a country in full development as a nation.
5.2. Second discourse: Champeta, as an experiment in authentic expansion
The narratives of champeta within the comparsa are diverse. Interestingly, among the comparsa
members who were interviewed, champeta is highlighted as an enlivener of the environment,
describing it as an entertaining, sexual, uninhibiting and even hipster genre. It could also be observed
that the positive valuation of champeta stands out especially among the newer members, something
that contrasts with those who have been members for more years. It is interesting to see how champeta
is often referred to in many different ways without any distinction: champeta africana, champeta

of cumbia by champeta. Some people diverged in the type of music they listened to, for example, the
same song was sometimes described as champeta, sometimes as Afro-Caribbean. In the
representations they show that champeta is very popular.
The representations of champeta are quite visible. On the one hand we see that cumbia shares much
of the comparsa's performances with what for many is called champeta. "Qué bonito", the Bomba
Estéreo song that for some punteros is considered a milestone for La Puntica No Má', is precisely a
champeta. The song mentions the comparsa and is played continuously in the concerts organized by
the comparsa. “Carnavalera”, the song by Bomba Estéreo and Systema Solar, also considered a
milestone for the comparsa, has musical elements that could relate it to the champeta. The comparsa
regularly invites to its events picoteros, whose main music is champeta. Wganda Kenya, the guest band
at the 2023 verbena, is known for performing songs that for some could be considered champeta.
It is said that the origin of the picós takes place in the 40's in the popular neighborhoods of the working
class (i.e. the lower strata) of Cartagena de Indias where they rented phonographs to listen and dance
with families and neighbors. In the 1950s these gatherings began to take place in spaces or dancehalls
called casetas (K-Zs), where there was a charge for entry and for the alcoholic beverages sold, which
they began to call picó (Birenbaum Quintero 2018). Competition among the picoteros, that is, the
owners of the picós29, was increasing, so they were very concerned about having the largest and
loudest system of amplifiers and speakers, but also about having repertoires that would attract more
customers. It is said that, in the beginning, what was played in the picós were mainly rural Colombian
musical practices that included cumbias, bullerengues and gaitas, as well as Cuban son music, learned
from Cuban engineers supervising the region`s sugar plantations in the 1920s (Minski, Ríos & Samper
2006). This forced the picoteros to interact with sailors who could help them obtain music and whom
they called corresponsales or "correspondents".
In the 70's picoteros discovered that the fanaticism of the customers could depend on a song that could
only be heard in a specific picó. These songs were called "exclusives" and their discovery depended on
the co-managers who had to look for records that were not easily identifiable by other picó.
Consequently, picoteros paid large sums of money for singular copies, or even for as many copies as
could be obtained, in order that another picó would not have them (Birenbaum Quintero 2018). This
competition brought about a change in repertoire, due to the fact that the correspondents were

The development of a repertoire selected on the basis of its exclusivity began to acquire a certain

music of Wganda Kenya can be understood as champetas, although of course, this statement can
generate debate. What is certain is that the champeta has a place in the comparsa and its members
have a series of imaginaries that are part of the discourse that underlies this genre within the comparsa.
These imaginaries, however, coincide with a rather constructivist type of discourse on authenticity,

5.2.1. First imaginary: The future of Colombian sound system movement
Champeta is revolutionizing the music scene, specifically the one related to the sound system
movement. Its appearance in various cultural contexts shows that it is increasingly appreciated and
enjoyed in spaces such as parties, parades or carnival festivals in Barranquilla. It is precisely in the
verbenas such as those organized by La Puntica that champeta acquires a leading role since in these
spaces there will always be a sound system or picó for its reproduction. And it is in these spaces where
the punteros have seen the musical expansion that the genre has achieved. In the text "Permutación
de la música de verbena: nuevas sonoridades. Caso Systema Solar" (2017), Piñeres and Vega Casanova
explain how verbena music, including champeta, presents new sonorities because of a cultural
hybridization that has been based on new processes of technological mediation and cultural
appropriation.
To begin with, Piñeres and Vega Casanova (2017) explain how technology has been key to the
reinvention of verbena music, starting with the incorporation of equipment and new production
techniques such as remixing songs. While this is not specific to champeta, in verbenas it takes on a
crucial role because the picoteros constantly test the type of equipment they use in the hope of
convincing a willing audience to judge the artist's new feats. Cardona, Muñoz, Álvarez and Velázquez
(2014) express that the human-tool interaction becomes so close that the mediating role of the tool
models both in the listeners' perceptions and in the performers' actions. Menin & Schiavio define
technological mediation from a musical point of view as to "the relationship between intentions and
desires on the part of musicians and the technology that generates the music" (2013 : 810). In other
words, technology serves as a mediator because it permanently allows the performer to evaluate
himself if he feels comfortable with the musical results obtained and the response of his audience.
To explain mediation by cultural appropriation, Piñeres and Vega Casanova refer to Subercaseaux
(1988) who establishes that this concept is associated with the idea of dependence and exogenous
domination, which points to a fertility and a creative process through which foreign elements become
"own" or "appropriate". For Piñeres and Vega Casanova, adaptations and transformations based on a
code initially different from their own become relevant because they imply construction dynamics that
can be musically valued as authentic if they are used in consonance with technological mediation. To
fully understand their point of view, let us understand that this study starts from the premise that these
new musics have had resistance to hegemonic sound manifestations that already sounded in the
carnival verbenas of Barranquilla and that, if it were not for these mediation processes added to an

-- 56 of 100 --

56
environment self-defined as "independent" that allows certain creative freedom, their affinity would
not have been achieved.
Champetas within the verbenas appeared much later than other genres such as traditional folkloric
music, salsa or vallenato. As Piñeres and Vega Casanova's text indicates, verbena music arrived even
later in the upper middle and upper strata, that is, where most of the members of La Puntica No Má'
live, as shown in the surveys carried out in this work. La Puntica could be considered an "independent"
space, so it is only natural that these musics flourish in verbenas organized by this comparsa.
Now, let's keep in mind that Systema Solar, the band that Piñeres and Vega Casanova's text exemplifies
as a case, is precisely one of the bands that La Puntica takes as a reference for one of its songs
("Carnavalera").It is worth noting that, in this article, the musicians themselves do not define their
music as champeta but rather as berbeneutika and argue that this music is based on Caribbean and
African music, where champeta itself is included, but its hybridization is generated with the contact of
electronic music such as house and techno. The term berbeneutika takes its name from the verbenas
but especially from a verbena party called "La Berbetrónika" which is still celebrated in Barranquilla
and where AfroBeat and music that it’s called today as champetas criollas [creole champetas] and
champetas africanas [African champetas] also flourish and still serve today as inspiration for the
creation of new champetas criollas. This party fulfills the same conditions that the Puntica has, that is
to say, a celebration where people of the upper and upper middle classes participate, willing to listen
to new sonorities given its "independent" character.
It is interesting to see that among the genres mentioned by the members of La Puntica, neither house
music, nor techno, much less berbeneutika were ever present, but there was a lot of talk about the
"champetas that Systema Solar makes". Perhaps, when the members of La Puntica indicate that the
future of the Colombian sound system movement is given thanks to the champeta, in reality what they
mean is that the singularity or authenticity of this process that is being developed is explained by this
type of sonorities that are being created thanks to a process of permutation that is found in contexts
similar to those of La Puntica. As explained above, the champeta was already danced in verbenas, but
only and exclusively in the verbenas of the popular neighborhoods of Barranquilla, but thanks to this
cultural hybridization, today it is allowed to be valued and recognized in a great part of the population,
breaking the barriers of social classes and reinventing itself as a musical manifestation.

members somehow expressed that champeta could make disappear or take away importance to
carnival music genres that have always accompanied this comparsa, including cumbia itself. The
concern is based on how champeta has been appearing in all carnival spaces in a fast and strong way,

An example can be the so-called Canción del Carnaval [Carnival Song], which as previously mentioned

capuchones or the letanías, which today have come back to life and which were traditions mentioned

Based on the above, the son de garabato could never be considered as elitelore because it is not a
manifestation that originally came from the upper classes, as were the waltz and the foxtrot, which
were always kept within the walls of private social clubs. Rather the son de garabato would be
considered as a resource of the folklore of the masses that was appropriated and could have all the
cultural apparatus in its favor due to the economic, political and social resources of the most influential
classes, but this never meant to overshadow other musical genres of the carnival.

59
The same could happen with champeta. One could consider the music of Wganda Kenya as a
manifestation of champeta and its "rebirth" as the fruit of the efforts of a comparsa like La Puntica No
Má', which may be related to the elites. Under Friedemann's parameters, this could be considered as
the appropriation of a folk music, but never a manifestation of elitelore. Wganda Kenya's music was
never forgotten within the context of carnival, however, it did take on a new impulse and meaning
within a comparsa, which sees in the past its source of inspiration to consolidate a creative future as it
is a musical genre that actively plays with the processes of globalization and its possibilities thanks to
technology. The latter never meant to displace other genres such as cumbia, in fact, more than 20 years
have passed since the first social networks were created and this has not happened, it has only been
strengthened to the same level as other carnival music genres already consolidated. In any case,
champeta has never been interested in displacing other genres either because in essence its industry
has always remained parallel to the mainstream and therefore never received influences from popular
genres among the masses of society such as vallenato (Birenbaum Quintero 2018). Perhaps, alternating
this genre with the others performed in this comparsa was the way in which these sonorities naturally
adjusted to the context of La Puntica.
It could be argued that experience has shown that the musical manifestations that originally came from
the elites were doomed to disappear, but those that were appropriated have endured or have been
revitalized and are strongly felt, however, none has managed to displace any other carnival music
genre.
5.2.3. Third imaginary: Between resistance and liberation of the bodies
Always when talking about champeta in the Colombian context it is common to find its association with
the black identity of the popular neighborhoods of cities like Cartagena or Barranquilla, which have
suffered rejection and discrimination by the elites who for a long time did not share their taste for this
musical genre. When reviewing texts related to champeta, many authors can be found who support
this idea based on historical facts of the colonial past, such as the transatlantic slave trade and places
like San Basilio de Palenque32, but also in what this genre means for the black communities of these
Colombian Caribbean cities that see in champeta the result of resistance struggles and their
reencounter with their origins in the African continent (Gómez Gómez 2022). Mosquera and Provansal
(2000), as well as Bohórquez (2000) explain that this aversion is mainly due to racial and moral
stereotypes that have long prevented the legitimacy of these manifestations.

department of Bolívar (Colombia). It was declared Cultural and Intangible Heritage of Humanity for being the first
free town in colonial America (so decreed in 1691); a place far from the colonies where African slaves escaped to
live in freedom, with autonomy and independence.

This historical exclusion has nevertheless had signs of rebellion and social protest that seek to proclaim
independence against the elites by embracing Africanness (Cueto Quintero 2016). In this way language,
types of relationships and other cultural manifestations such as champeta are elements that on the
one hand are seen as tools whose purpose is to protect memory and highlight Afro identities in the
Colombian Caribbean, but on the other hand transgress the socioeconomic, political and aesthetic
values that another part of society had already cemented (Del Río 2003). Cunin (2003) explains that
the way of perceiving these claims as an aggression is typical of the construction of the Afro as the
"other", interpreted as the savage, the "non-white", the disorderly, the illegal, the vicious and the
"uncivilized". As a consequence, its invisibilization and rejection has been promoted, motivating the
historical aversion towards champeta.
There are several elements of the champeta that may clash with already established customs, such as
the informal market product of the exchange typical of the global south that was built (Birenbaum
Quintero 2018) and the use of a language defined as "champetúo" (Aldana 2006). However, the
champeta dance is undoubtedly one of the elements that has presented the most disapproval. Its high
erotic and sexual content has been seen as a threat to the hegemonic sectors that have been formed
with conservative and traditionalist discourses with the body (Bohórquez 2000).
In the text Champeta/terapia: más que música y moda, folclor urbanizado del Caribe colombiano
[Champeta/terapia: more than just music and fashion, urbanized folklore of the Colombian Caribbean]
by Contreras Hernández (2003), certain unthinkable resistances identified in the context of this musical
genre are discussed. According to him, the verbenas and champetas are spaces where in some way
machismo has experienced erosion. He affirms that with the champeta there has been greater
autonomy and participation of women, especially in terms of socializing and dancing. He continues his
argument by stating that this sexual dance does not necessarily constitute the labeling of women as
promiscuous or vulgar, but rather that they have been able to embrace their freedom and can be
admired for their dancing skills. In principle, the dance demands a closeness between the couples in
which they should be practically embracing, intertwining their legs and touching the pelvis of the
dancing partner. Contreras Hernández (2003) defines this basic position as "dance embrace". The same
author lists a variety of dance passes that invite one to forget modesty and celebrate the bodies, for
example: el reloj [the clock], el cochero [the coachman], el policía [the policeman], la tijera [the
scissors], el taxista [the taxi driver], la camita [the little bed], el escorpión [the scorpion], la pulga [the
flea], etc.
This is perhaps the reason why the members of La Puntica No Má' see in this rhythm a way to rebel
and be uninhibited regardless of the social norms that in Barranquilla and Colombian society may not

5.3. Third discourse: As a virgin everything is authentic
As a new member or virgin puntero it is inevitable to listen to all kinds of music in La Puntica No Má',
although cumbia and champeta are undoubtedly the two musical genres that are most perceived. The
concerts, the parties, the parades... everything sounds and everything is extraordinary. This feeling of
novelty and wonder with each of the sounds that are reproduced in the comparsa is undoubtedly one
of the most impressive elements. This mainly impacts foreigners and people from other regions of
Colombia who have never been to a Carnival before and who see in the comparsa a different way of
experiencing Carnival. However, my experience as a researcher, one who comes from the city of
Barranquilla, made me think that perhaps this feeling also extends to Colombians from the Caribbean
and to the Barranquilleros themselves who see in La Puntica a comparsa that takes risks not only in
what has to do with the costumes but also in terms of sound. When reviewing the interviews and the
notes on conversations with other virgin punteros, local or not, it can be verified that the feeling was
shared and that all described it as something magical, that does not have a defined meaning, only
admiration, surprise and fascination for the novelty.
While I knew many of the rhythms and songs that were played at the comparsa events, the way each
individual or the collective relates to each musical sample is different. The meanings and codes that
these songs have within the comparsa were unknown to me, being a local. Also, it is not the same to
know a song like "Qué bonito" by Bomba Estéreo on the radio, as it is to hear it for the first time during
a La Puntica No Má' event, followed by a drum roll playing cumbias. It was strange to me that in the
same comparsa two musical genres with such different formats were performed and danced
simultaneously as La Puntica No Má' does, since the norm in the Carnival of Barranquilla is that its
comparsas appropriate only one musical genre or several that have similar characteristics such as the
format of instrumentation. In this sense, the musical approach of the motto "Tradition in evolution" as
understood by La Puntica No Má' is an exception.

What is certain is that in order to identify and internalize the discourses that these two musical genres
may have within the comparsa, a first moment of encounter with them is necessary. This first
encounter, which is difficult to describe, but which can be defined as a unique experience about an
environment that is just getting to know can indicate what Wesener (2017), Kim, Oh, Lee & Lee (2018)
and Yi, Lin, Jin & Luo (2017) call experiential authenticity. This is because the meanings of the stories
(narratives) and their performances (representations) that one did not have or previously had about
certain elements, of which one even already has some knowledge, collide with an alternate version of
them, resulting in something novel and authentic.
This shock is not really related to a specific musical genre, in fact it can be any one that has been
listened to, it can be with a cumbia, or a champeta, or any other musical genre that has had an initial
impact, and it is emphasized that it is initial because with time and with the full understanding of the
meanings of each sound material this fades away.
5.4. Music as discursive masking in a carnival troupe full of masks
We have seen how the narratives and performances of La Puntica No Má' were crucial to identify two
musical genres that are associated with two discourses on authenticity, while the latter was identified
by a first impression or total ignorance or clash with the meanings of the stories (narratives) and their
performances (representations) that the music as a whole might have within the comparsa. What is
striking here is that these three discourses participate within the same context, which is the comparsa
of La Puntica No Má', and that all the members must at some point have embodied each of the three
discourses, of course always starting with the last discourse described here. The use of the term
"incarnate" is also striking due to the nature of the group that is being investigated in this work, a
comparsa of costumes where there are many costumes and masks that embody characters that appear
once they are used by the members of the group.
It is for this reason that this paper proposes the term "musical discursive masks" to describe these
discursive positions that are promoted through a type of music, in a context where individuals
sometimes embody one of these discursive masks, ignoring the others, with the possibility of
abandoning it to adjust to some other and later, if necessary, to take it up again. The characteristics of
the "musical discursive masking" are:
1) They participate in the same system and are recognized under different aesthetic and musical
parameters.
2) They have discourses, guided by narratives and representations, which have been shaped by
a complex process of globalization.

-- 64 of 100 --

64
3) within the same system they can dialogue with each other, support each other or, on the
contrary, contradict each other.
4) they are temporary, they are interchangeable.
5) all members of the system have embodied them in some way at some time.
In the system of La Puntica No Má' there would be three musical discursive masking of authenticity:
• Virgin masking: It is related to the experiential discourse on authenticity, in which the first
experience of the individual is perceived as an authentic fact. It is mainly experienced by virgin
or new punteros who are just confronted with certain sonorities whose meanings of this
comparsa they have not yet internalized.
• Masking of cumbia: It is related to an essentialist discourse on authenticity linked to the
musical genre of cumbia. It is based on that approach towards the pure, the unique and the
ancestral, whose value lies in highlighting its multiethnic origins and its precursor function,
under the premise that the roots articulate the comparsa and its sonorities. It conveys a
rejection of anything that could erase these values. From the perspective of globalization, it
can be said that this discursive masking is sustained by narratives that refer to its first two
stages ("formation" and part of the "institutionalization") that are related to the carnival as it
is explained as a consequence of a miscegenation typical of the colonial era and of a strong
process of classification (folklore studies), protection and safeguarding (heritage studies), and
legitimization (institutions) that seek to give meaning to all kinds of reasons for its value. It
could also be said that this discourse goes through representations that seek to recall the
beginnings of the comparsa and its inspiration for the creation of La Puntica No Má'.
• Masking of champeta: It is related to a constructivist discourse on authenticity linked to the
musical genre of champeta. It is based on that approach towards change, towards the new and
the modern. It sees in dynamism its greatest value, considering it as something singular, as well
as in the capacity to reinvent itself. It embodies the breaking of social, political, economic and
aesthetic structures, the vindication of what makes society uncomfortable and the
appropriation of new forms for the creation of innovative proposals. From the perspective of
globalization, this discursive masking is supported by the narratives of the last two stages (part
of the "institutionalization" and integration) that are related to the carnival, as it questions the
way in which the structures of society were cemented (mainly in the narratives within the
Academy) and communicates and feeds aesthetically and ideologically from a series of
interconnected nodes that lie in various parts of the world, thanks to advances in technology.
On the other hand, this discourse could be understood as circulating through representations
that explore the future of the comparsa, new generations and freedom of expression.
It should be noted that the last two masks present tension, since they contradict each other in what
they seek to promote while being in the same comparsa. On the one hand, it could be said that the
masking of cumbia does not align with the masking of champeta because its appearance invites to
explore the external and the new. On the other hand, the masking of champeta might not coincide
with the masking of cumbia because the latter closes itself to what is already written and seeks to find
inspiration in the past. The truth is that when we review the history of each of these two genres we
see that the previous ideas are not entirely true. Cumbia has had its own evolution and has known how

verbena party of La Puntica in 2023. Which would indicate that these maskings could have been

6. Conclusions
In this paper we have seen how, through a mixed methodology, it has been possible to verify that for
La Puntica No Má', a collective costume troupe of the Carnival of Barranquilla, there are two musical
genres that stand out in their narratives and musical representations: cumbia and champeta. A
triangulation analysis of these narratives and representations allowed us to discover which discourses
on authenticity were transmitted through these musical genres. The discourses in this context were
presented as "musical discursive masking", which are discursive positions on the same topic promoted
by certain musical genres and which are characterized because a) they are recognized under different
aesthetic and musical parameters within the same system; b) they have discourses, guided by
narratives and representations, that have been shaped by a complex process of globalization; c) within
the same system they can dialogue with each other, support each other or, on the contrary, contradict
each other; d) they are temporary and interchangeable, and e) all members of the group have
embodied them in some way at some time.
This research identified three musical discursive maskings: the cumbia, the champeta and the virgin.
Each is associated with each of the three types of discourses on authenticity (essentialist, constructivist

-- 66 of 100 --

66
and experiential) described by Salge Ferro (2020). The masking of cumbia advocates an essentialist
discourse on authenticity that values ancestry as unique and origins as pure. From the perspective of
globalization, it has a strong relationship with the first narratives that were told about carnival, i.e.
those that talk about the creation of carnival and tried to explain it based on studies of folklore, heritage
and institutions. His transit through the representations is perceived in the way in which he presents
himself as an asset that seeks to recall the beginnings of the comparsa and his inspiration for the
creation of La Puntica No Má'.
In the masking of champeta, the constructivist discourse on authenticity that understands the process
as the most precious for its uniqueness shines through. From the perspective of globalization, this
discursive masking is supported by narratives of the last two stages (part of the "institutionalization"
and integration) of the carnival, as it reviews the way in which the structures of society were molded
and the role of technology in it. On the other hand, it could be understood that this discourse circulates
through representations that explore the future of the comparsa, the new generations and freedom of
expression. Finally, there is the virgin masking where the experiential discourse on authenticity
highlights the clash between preconceived ideas about certain elements, and their arrangement and
meanings within the comparsa.
This research shows how these musical discursive maskings can exist within the same comparsa,
despite their contradictions. These incongruities coincide with the classical theory of carnival proposed
by Bakhtin (Bakhtin 1987; Lachmann 1988-89) in the sense that the existence of a polyphony of voices
that distance themselves from reality through the superimposition of dualities that do not function in
everyday life ("the grotesque", in Bakhtin's words) is perceived. For this, the differentiation by periods
was very key thanks to the use of the globalization approach. In this way, it was possible to have a
broader vision of the emergence of certain narratives and representations.
On the other hand, the masking discourses show how the discourses, when divided into imaginaries
and investigated, show historical flaws, lack of evidence and generalizations that have been
accumulating. We see how the antiquity of the participation of millo in the carnival in the case of
cumbia or the innovative character of champeta, which can make use of songs from past decades, can
be easily questioned.
The discursive masking in turn allowed us to see how accumulated discourses have continued to shape
narratives and representations during carnival. In the midst of a struggle between discourses that
understand authenticity as a good to be protected and those that advocate change as the ultimate
expression of the authentic, the carnival leaves no evidence that during the days of the festivities the
essence of the social, economic and political structures of a city like Barranquilla changes temporarily.

-- 67 of 100 --

67
Sometimes the opposite happens, revealing unpopular practices for some, such as cultural
appropriation, but it has also promoted new positions such as the recognition of certain identities. In
this way creation and reinterpretation are mixed creating new musical proposals for the future
exploring those of the past, in a more conscious and critical way in front of the old hegemonic
configurations.
As a final reflection, it was asked whether there could be discursive masking in other types of events
other than the one presented here. An example could be the concerts and parades organized by the
Colombian government to commemorate Colombia's independence every year on July 20. On this day,
different sound samples can be played, from pop music to the National Anthem of Colombia, including
folk music from each of the regions of Colombia. In this case there may be clashing discourses related
to the concept of independence: on the one hand, there will be those who could adjust to the discursive
masking of marches and the National Anthem, with postures that advocate independence as a
consequence of a legitimate use of force and the imposition of sovereignty, on the other hand, there
will be those who adjust to the masking of traditional music, who understand independence as the full
development of cultural differences, and there will also be the masking of pop, with followers that
understand independence as the feeling of spontaneity and lack of stress. All of the above, of course,
are assumptions that require additional research work.
