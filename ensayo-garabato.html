Resumen

La costa Caribe colombiana es una de las regiones más desiguales del país, y su mayor fiesta, el Carnaval de Barranquilla, refleja tanto su cultura como sus diferencias socioeconómicas. A partir de Bourdieu, Marx y el nacionalismo romántico, el ensayo sigue la historia del garabato: una danza de origen esclavo, bailada por primera vez en Rebolo en 1861, que desde 1937 adoptaron los clubes de la élite y que hoy encabeza el Garabato del Country. Analiza cómo las clases altas estilizaron su música, su vestuario y su coreografía, y cómo la radio y la prensa la impusieron como himno con Te olvidé. Concluye que las élites «bajaron de estrato» en dos épocas —para ganar un lugar en la nación y, tras la declaratoria de la Unesco en 2003, para venderse al mundo— y que el sistema de estratos se trasladó de lo económico a lo cultural.

Abstract

Colombia’s Caribbean coast is one of the most unequal regions of the country, and its largest festival, the Carnival of Barranquilla, reflects both its culture and its socio-economic divides. Drawing on Bourdieu, Marx and Romantic Nationalism, the essay follows the history of the garabato: a dance of enslaved origin, first danced in Rebolo in 1861, adopted from 1937 by the elite clubs and now at the head of the Garabato del Country parade. It examines how the upper classes stylised its music, costumes and choreography, and how radio and the press established it as an anthem with Te olvidé. It concludes that the elites “downgraded” their estrato in two epochs —to win a place in the nation and, after UNESCO’s 2003 proclamation, to sell themselves to the world— and that the estrato system moved from the economic to the cultural.

Zusammenfassung

Die kolumbianische Karibikküste gehört zu den ungleichsten Regionen des Landes, und ihr größtes Fest, der Karneval von Barranquilla, spiegelt sowohl ihre Kultur als auch ihre sozioökonomischen Gegensätze. Ausgehend von Bourdieu, Marx und dem romantischen Nationalismus verfolgt der Essay die Geschichte des Garabato: ein Tanz versklavter Herkunft, 1861 erstmals in Rebolo getanzt, seit 1937 von den Clubs der Elite übernommen und heute an der Spitze des Umzugs Garabato del Country. Er untersucht, wie die Oberschicht Musik, Kostüme und Choreografie stilisierte und wie Radio und Presse ihn mit Te olvidé als Hymne durchsetzten. Fazit: Die Eliten sind in zwei Epochen „im Estrato abgestiegen“ – um einen Platz in der Nation zu gewinnen und, nach der UNESCO-Erklärung von 2003, um sich der Welt zu verkaufen –, und das Estrato-System hat sich vom Ökonomischen ins Kulturelle verlagert.

Colombia's Caribbean coast is home to many of the country's most colorful and recognizable traditions. It is also the site of power dynamics that have been passed down from generation to generation and make this region one of the most unequal in Colombia (UNDP 2021). The Carnival of Barranquilla is the largest festival in the country, received more than million visitors in 2019 (Cotelco 2019), and reflects perfectly the most outstanding of the culture of the Colombian Caribbean and at the same time the socio-economic differences that characterize the region. Its music and dances have been a seal of identity for different population niches, but also an object of appropriation by others with more power in the social hierarchy. This essay aims to explain how class dynamics work in Barranquilla's Carnival through the review of the case of one of its dances called garabato and how morality has played an important role in maintaining or adjusting the behavior of members of different social classes in the city by shaping customs, interactions, and aesthetic tastes.

Reviewing Bourdieu’s statements about aesthetic disposition, taste, and symbolic violence (Fuller 2008); Marx’s thinking on class struggle (Muravchik 2002; Peña 2019) and the Romantic Nationalism evidenced by Wilson (1973) and Peña (1985), the idea is to answer the following question: Can class dynamics influence the music trends for each edition of Barranquilla’s Carnival in 21st century? It will also be useful to revise the writings on the identity construction of Barranquilla through its music during carnival times by Wade (2000), what is understood by social stratification in Colombia (Fuller 2008), and audiovisual content hosted on social networks such as YouTube.

To begin with, I am from Barranquilla. I was born in the late 80’s and participated in each edition of the Carnival since my childhood, even when I moved in 2007 to Bogotá, the capital city of Colombia, to continue my studies in Music and Sound Engineering. For us Barranquilleros it is impossible to live in the same country and not return every year to enjoy the carnival. In general terms, the carnival changed little: the same music, the same events, the same party. However, I started living in Bogotá, a city where Costeños (people from the Colombian Caribbean coast) were always judged as people with very marked differences in terms of class and race. Of course, I began to realize this every time I returned to Barranquilla and noticed that many Barranquilleros were not able to see the strong class dynamics that existed in the city. Some even said that they could exist but during carnival, these dynamics of class disappeared. I don't think these dynamics disappear; they are just readjusted.

It is important to clarify certain concepts. In Barranquilla, as in many places in Colombia, class and race are closely linked (Conde 2009). In one hand, the definition of class that we take in this essay is related to the distinctions in practices and values that result from a division of society from different economic, political, and social points of view. This categorization can be seen in the works of Marx and Weber. (Saunders 1990). According to Marx's work, the means of production are the set of technologies and forms of social organization through which people satisfy their material needs and that their conception generates divisions in society. For Marx there is a fundamental and seminal distinction, the society’s socio-economic base, that is the mode of production predominant in the society, and the cultural and ideological superstructure, in other words, the set of beliefs and practices circulating in that society. Marx believed that the base ultimately informs the superstructure, and thus, the mode of production in material life determines the general character of the social, political, and spiritual processes of life. Peña states that capitalism, viewed as a specific epoch in the history, comprises a social and economic system, where the cultural superstructure is a heterogenous entity encompassing a range of practices and beliefs, including both “residual” elements that linger on from past historical periods and newly “emergent” ones (2019).

On the other hand, race is defined as a social and cultural category which contained a series of differentiating and stigmatizing characteristics and symbols due to physical features of individuals and collectives such as skin color (Naranjo 2017; Vásquez-Padilla 2017). Since colony times, black people and indigenous communities in Colombia have lived violence and lack of opportunities in comparison to the white upper classes of the society (Cunin, 2003; Pérez, 2017; Tusa, Briceño & Tusa 2016). This was one of the consequences of the Triangular Trade established between 17th and 19th centuries among the American, European, and African continents (Naranjo 2017) where white people held the power and black and indigenous people were enslaved to provide services. For this reason, class should be understood as a term that can sometimes be intertwined with the category of race; however, this essay will discuss and analyze the case of study only from approaches related to the class dynamics and morality.

To answer the question "Can class dynamics influence the music trends for each edition of Barranquilla's Carnival in 21st century" it is necessary to address the adjustments in the legislative framework in Colombia in the 90’s that determine certain contemporary class dynamics in the country. The Colombian Congress, through article 102 of Law 142 of July 11, 1994 (Law establishing the regime of domiciliary public utilities and other provisions) created a public taxing system that is unique to South America and to the world. It operates through a socio-economic stratification that divides cities in six urban areas, assigned as estratos. The idea was to insure a fair redistributive system between the rich and the poor people. In principle, the classification is based on the condition of the street in front of a property, construction materials and access to public services and transportation, but ended up reproducing inequality.

As Fully (2008) states, the emergence of the stratos were originally an objective administrative measure, that blended into class conceptions. She explains: “Today, social stratification shapes the daily lives of Colombians dividing society into six categories. This phenomenon has problematic consequences such as increasing social segregation and decreasing social mobility. But what is the most interesting from a sociological point of view, is how society accepted and consented to this socio-economic organisation, which is seen as ‘normal’. This issue raises questions about social class representations and processes of consent and power. Thus, Colombian society openly displays stratification and concedes to a hierarchical social order” (Fuller 2008:2).

Estratos also affects the system of values of the classes, the artistic tastes, and the entertainment consumption, and create imaginaries among the different estratos that are right now very naturalize and demarcated. The above may be a consequence of the processes that each estrato have lived for more than three decades that end up sharing similar elements such as the urban landscape and relationships with their environment, and the identity values (Iriarte Diazgranados 2012), and that now emphasize the problems or privileges that are the result of shortages or abundance, and the standards of beauty and morality (Ángel Moreno 2016). Today, any behavior that breaks the parameters of the estratos is mocked with irony or criticized with distaste. It is common to hear phrases such as se te notó el estrato (look, how you show your estrato) to denote how your manners is not according to your “own” estrato; no traiciones a tu estrato (do not betray your estrato) to embarrass someone who wants to change his “own” estrato, even if the person in question ‘is’ estrato 1, 3 or 6, and the expression subir or bajar de estrato (estrato upgrade or downgrade), to sign that there has been a sudden change in behavior or that people unexpectedly have money or lost it.

In an interview broadcast on Youtube and conducted by Cristina Amortegui, a journalist and presenter associated to the radio station La Voz de la Costa of Barranquilla, she asks the audience in attendance what is the first thing they imagine when the word garabato is mentioned to them (2018). Garabato is a dance that is presumed to be original from the enslaved blacks and now is considered one of the most traditional and popular dances of the Barranquilla’s Carnival. The first answer is curiously Parque Washington or Washington Park, that is a park located about Altos del Prado, a neighborhood considered estrato 6, which means the highest (and wealthiest) estrato. To understand this answer, it is important to clarify that one of the Pre-Carnival events (a series of activities prior to Carnival that serve to generate expectation) is the Garabato del Country, a parade that is very popular and whose meeting place is the Washington Park. The question then would be: How is a dance of slave origin today the protagonist of one of the most important pre-carnival parades located in one of the richest neighborhoods of the city?

It is known that the garabato dance is a black folkloric expression that was possibly transferred by animators of popular festivities to the Carnival from Ciénaga, a town in the Caribbean Department of Magdalena, near Barranquilla. The dance is from the same family as the Torito and Congo dances, and in it is staged, with irony and sarcasm, the struggle between life and death (Oviedo 2019) or as the local cultural manager, Humberto Pernett, says, the struggle between joy against sadness (La Fiesta es de Todos 2017). According to Oviedo, the dance was used by the black slaves of the banana plantations to mock their masters, their own misfortune, nature, work, the gods, death, and everything they encountered in their path, and it was danced during parties or sacred festivities -generally Candlemas- when the masters granted them days of rest (2019).

Garabato was danced in Barranquilla for the first time in 1861 in Rebolo, a estrato-1-and-2 neighborhood (Oviedo 2019; El Tiempo 2011). At the beginning of the 20th century the dance was revived by Sebastián Mesura, a cultural manager of Rebolo, and, from 1929, included by José Terán Mesa in the group of dances that during the carnival toured the popular neighborhoods of the city. According to Humberto Pernett, Terán died in a fight on Carnival Saturday in 1947 and many believed that with his death garabato would also die. However, the following year Casto de la Hoz, Adolfo Villalba and other people paraded in the carnival dancing the garabato dance (Oviedo 2019).

In 1937, Luis Vives Polanco and Soledad Román de Vives motivated a group of young people belonging to the social clubs of the city to dance garabato during the Carnival of Barranquilla. According to the anthropologist Nina Friedemann, one of those young people was Emiliano Vengoechea Diazgranados, who is responsible for this dance being adopted by the Vengoechea family and the Country Club of Barranquilla. Since then, several generations of members of that club have danced garabato and the Vengoechea family has assumed the direction of the “Garabato de Emiliano” dance troupe with Emiliano as its head until his death in 1990, and his children Ricardo, until 1997, and Luis Fernando, to this day. That is why today garabato is considered by many Barranquilleros as the dance of the rich people (Oviedo 2019), even though Luis Fernando Vengoechea reaffirms that garabato is for all social classes (El Heraldo 2016).

The above can be explained by Pierre Bourdieu. He states that individual inherit class though family and education, with a set of practices and representations which allow the expression and reproduction of their social class (Fuller 2008). This group of elements, also called “cultural capital”, are actual advantages that can accompany the acquisition of education and aesthetic sophistication and can be used by the well-educated elite as a sign of social superiority (Peña 2019). In this way, garabato became an element of the cultural capital of the members of the Country Club of Barranquilla; the knowledge about this genre and its transfer from generation to generation a social indicator.

Oviedo describes the choreography of the dance as a movement of the wave at marching pace (2019). In a video published by the Country Club's official channel, eight basic steps of the dance can be observed, these are: Seguidilla, Lado a lado, Desplazamiento lateral, Adelante y atrás, Variante del desplazamiento lateral, Gancho, Túnel and Palito (2016). These very stylized dance steps are interrupted by the character of La Muerte (The Dead) who threatens the life, represented by the caporal or leader of the dance troupe, who is always a male dancer (Olímpica 2017; Parra 2021). With a wooden stick in the shape of a hook with yellow, green, and red ribbons hung on it, the colors of the flag of Barranquilla, the caporal attacks La Muerte who responds with his scythe until it is defeated. The name of this wooden stick is garabato and from there comes the name of the dance.

In addition to the garabato, the actual costumes worn by the men consist of a long-sleeved yellow shirt, a bib-shaped blue breastplate with lace and embroidered with sequins, black pants with lace knee pads, high white stockings (although the caporal has stockings of other colors so as not to confuse), black shoes, red cape adorned with sequins and figures of a thousand colors, white hat adorned with ribbons in the colors of the flag of Barranquilla and the flower of the Chinese hibiscus (Hibiscus rosa-sinensis). The women wear a long black skirt and a black blouse with a wide neckline, both topped with ruffles with the colors of Barranquilla’s flag, black shoes and in her hair an ornament of Chinese hibiscus flowers (Universidad Autónoma del Caribe 2020). The cost of the dress is usually very high, compared to other carnival costumes due to the luxuries added by the upper classes to the original disguise (Oviedo 2019; Universidad Autónoma del Caribe 2020). The changes proposed by the upper classes to the original aesthetics of the different elements of garabato also respond to processes described by Bourdieu. In Distinction, he argues that individuals are aesthetically disposed: rules of beauty and good taste are inherently learnt and associated to social class (1994). Thus, a stylized cultural manifestation is a clear rule of beauty and good taste imposed and normalized. Today it is impossible to appreciate garabato costumes without those ornaments.

At first, the garabato was danced only by men to the sound of the alegre drum (a cylindrical idiophone) and the guacharaca (an idiophone). There is divergence in the musical rhythm, some say it is a chandé, others say it is a (son de) pajarito (Oviedo 2019; Burgos 2019). In videos analyzed with the rhythm of son de pajarito (Escorcia 2021; Pirotecnia Colectivo Creativo 2013), there are coincidences with the current garabato rhythm, such as the rhythmic pattern of the alegre and llamador drums, as well as that of the shakers, which can be maracas or guache. However, what most characterizes the musical rhythm that is recognized for the current garabato dance are the two deep and out-of-phase beats on the tambora drum in the last pulses of the binary meter (₵) in which it is written (López & Martínez 2015; Burgos 2019). These beats function as the sounds that announce the arrival of the garabato dance troupes and are easily recognized by the carnival Barranquilleros. These deep hits on the drumhead do appear in the chandé described by Victoriano Valencia Rincón in Pitos y tambores. Cartilla de iniciación musical (2004), a manual produced by the Colombian Ministry of Culture in which the rhythmic patterns of some musical genres of the western Colombian Caribbean are transcribed. However, the chandé described by Valencia has 3 beats instead of two and they are not out of phase as they are heard today in the music danced by the the garabato dance troups. In addition, it is quite common to see garabatos danced with orchestrated versions with wind, piano and string instruments, a format more like the big bands. For these reasons we could say that the music danced in garabato is a stylized mixture of chandé and son de pajarito. It is also not bad to recognize this genre as a new one, in fact, folklorists such as percussionists Orito Cantora (2020), Roberto Camargo (2020) and Toño Burgos (2019).

Up to this point, garabato as dance, music and clothing has undergone changes that have been produced by its adoption by the upper classes. However, explaining the reasons why garabato was appropriated by the upper classes requires an analysis that invites us to go back to the beginning of the 20th century, when this genre was rejected because of its slave origins. In Music, race and nation: Tropical music in Colombia, Peter Wade explains the changes in the consumption of the music for the different social classes in Barranquilla between 1920 and 1950 (2000). He states that in the first part of the 20th century the middle class and, mostly, the elite preferred European music and rhythms from the center of the country, like pasillos and bambucos that were also associated with the European music. However, the low class usually listened to local music interpreted with homemade instruments or brass ensembles, seen as vulgar and inferior by the elite. In the group of these genres could be included garabato music.

Cuban, American, Mexican, Argentinian, and Brazilian music started to be popular for the high classes because of the import of recordings and the invitation of international orchestras and big bands that played those types of music in private clubs, specially, during the editions of Barranquilla’s Carnival of the 1930s. One of those clubs was precisely the Country Club, founded on December 27, 1926. Soon, all these foreign music was adopted by the middle and low classes. Thereafter, newly founded radio stations and record labels found economic opportunities with this new market and created their own orchestras and big bands with local musicians, who gradually included traditional Costeño music (Continental Colombian-Caribbean music). Nevertheless, the music presented was a refined, sophisticated version of the traditional porros, cumbias and other genres that were familiar to the low classes. There were restrictions for the musicians, who should be white or light-skinned professionals or members of the elite, and to the repertoire, who should maintain the morals, the decency, the family stability, and the behavior of the society, with no offensive or double-entendre lyrics.

These strong class dynamics invite us to reflect on Marx's work that brought to the scholars in the middle of the 19th century new points of view of how to understand history and its processes. One of its collaborators, Friedrich Engel, embraced the term building a cult around it and defending that “the history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggle” (Muravchik 2002). In other words, the history of societies depends on struggles of social organizations to control the means of production and media, and to impose specific ideologies. Those who succeed will define the characteristics associated with each epoch or the changes that allow the transition to a new one (Peña 2019). In the case of Barranquilla at the beginning of the 20th century, the upper classes were defining norms of behavior and adjusting certain aesthetic patterns to be taken into account by the other classes.

The reason why garabato reached the ears of Luis Vives Polanco and Soledad Román de Vives in 1937 and convinced a group of young people from upper classes is not very clear. However, everything points to the fact that garabato dance troupes from the Rebolo neighborhood, organized by José Terán Mesa, ended during the carnival at Paseo Bolívar, a place near Barranquilla Club, another of the city's elite clubs during the early 20th century, where during some social visit they could somehow appreciate and get the idea of adopting the rhythm. The truth is that garabato became a symbol of the upper classes, with highly stylized musical examples such as Te olvidé [I forgot you] composed by maestro Antonio María Peñaloza Cervantes (1954). With its catchy music and its lyrics, which highlight the greatness of a person who loves deeply and is heartbroken, Te olvidé was recorded by Sonora Curro and its popularity in Barranquilla, after 66 years, is largely due to the radio and its great broadcasting work. In fact, Miguel Iriarte, director of Barranquilla’s “Piloto del Caribe” Library, explains that Antonio María Peñaloza adjusts the rhythmic patterns of the percussion instruments in Te olvidé and thereby creates the rhythm of son de garabato as it is currently danced (Uni5 TV 2016).

Te olvidé is also important because is considered by many as the anthem of Barranquilla’s Carnival since its first reproduction in 1954. It should be noted that Antonio María Peñaloza's music was not only played on the radio but also in presentations at clubs such as the Country Club and the Barranquilla Club (Solano Alonso & Bassi Labarrera 2016). Te olvidé, being a son de garabato, fits perfectly with the values of Barranquilla's clubs. The members of these clubs who also had connections with the record and radio industry may have unintentionally or intentionally imposed this music as a banner for what should be considered an anthem for the city's most outstanding celebration. According to Bourdieu, society consents to an impose hierarchy of cultural taste which is seen as normal. Thus, symbolic violence can be defined as the mechanism through which dominant groups establish a cultural system which is commonly accepted as natural and legitimate through a conscious or unconscious (intentional or unintentional) implementation of explicit or implicit schemes of perception (Fuller 2008).

Those who dominate the mass media can impose specific ideologies. In a natural sense, ideologies are a set of meanings, values aesthetics, expectations, socio-political attitudes, and ways of conceiving the self and society in a culture, but in Marxist thought it can have a more specific meaning referring to the “set of ideas [that] serves to legitimize, naturalize, and perpetuate the socio-economic hegemony exerted by the dominant class” (Peña 2019). These “hegemonic ideologies” can be effectively invisible, pervasive, and unquestioned, even when they would stand in sharp contrast to the prevailing worldview. If for some reason there are modifications to these hegemonic ideologies, it can be said that there are signs of an epochal change. In Barranquilla in the third decade of the twentieth century, a change took place from an aesthetic point of view, which meant a new era.

This change is analyzed by Wade who argues that Barranquillas’s most affluent and upwardly-mobile classes found itself in an ambivalent position in relation with the changes of the musical scene, but they were also trying to develop an image of modernity and prosperity for the region and the nation (Hernández 2015), because costeños (people from the Caribbean coast) felt themselves politically and culturally discriminated. The dominant classes considered that music can certainly help to build that desired image far from the sonorities of Andean pasillos and bambucos, in an exercise of creating an authentic face for the city, even though, at that time, Barranquilla was a thriving not-colonial young city in contrast to other towns of the country and even more cosmopolitan than the Colombian capital city of Bogotá because of the considerable number of foreigners that received Barranquilla since its inception (Wade 2000).

Wade argues that local music could represent the perfect, but also unstable, balance between tradition and modernity because music could be susceptible to multiple readings conditioned by different perspectives. Music was central in the social life of the high class and the wealthiest people of Barranquilla invest a lot of time, effort and money to uphold their very exclusive entertainment events and business. Therefore, it was mandatory for the elite to monitor the appropriation process of the local music that was imbuing all the layers of the society, including their private clubs and the Carnival, that was controlled by them. The educated middle class wished to participate in the urban cultural life of the city, adding diversity, legitimacy and relevance to the events, and becoming consumers of those services produced by the upper class of Barranquilla. That’s why instead a conflict, there was a tacit alliance between them and the elite, inheriting basic hegemonic values, linked to racial and class hierarchy.

According to Peña (2019), there are a lot of examples of class struggles in music. For instance, the Lumpen proletariat -or the lowest class related to driftlers, hustlers or buskers- has placed a role in the creation of various music genres, like rebetika in Greece, tango in Argentina, dancehall in Jamaica and korcong in Indonesia, as a means of reaffirming their identity, senses of community and aesthetics in contexts where other classes (the proletariat -or the working class, wage-earning and blue-collars workers- and the bourgeoisie -or the dominant class that owns the means of production-) are not willing to give up space for its diffusion. However, these tensions can also exist between the proletariat and the bourgeoisie. One clear example of this type of tension is explained in Manuel Peña’s The Texas-Mexican Conjunto: History of a Working-Class Music (1985), where accordion-based conjunto music of the proletarian Mexican-American population competed against the more mainstream American-based orquesta music of the upwardly-mobile Tejanos, who aspired to be recognized for their commitment to the U.S. nation and their economic achievements.

Peña argued that both formats (conjunto and orquesta) are consequence of the Romantic Nationalism, specifically related to lo mexicano -the elements that reaffirms the collective sense of Mexican identity- and the symbols underlaying it: Virgin of Guadalupe, the Aztec Warrior, the charro or vaquero that symbolizes the arrogant manliness (machismo) and the campesino (1985). In this sense, Romantic Nationalism tries to rescue and enhance those mores that are considered authentic, unique, pure, and even vernacular (Abrahams, 1993). Johann Gottfried Herder was the founder of this movement, and he described a set of tenets, for example, the existence of different historical ages in nations, which forms an independent cultural entity, whose parts are integrally related to form an organic whole and the necessity of every nationality to develop in accord with its own innate abilities, geographical environment, and innate cultural elements.

In his paper Herder, folklore and romantic nationalism, Wilson (1973) explained how the “marriage” of folklore research and nationalistic endeavors occurred, and described some of its results, such as the study of folklore items as survivals from the past, that turned into a model for the development of some nations and the inspiration of other dependent nations seeking historical justification for their separatist policies, through the romantic nationalism movement. This movement has repercussions in the Lomax family that had the interest of traveling thousands of miles across the United States making thousands of recordings looking for “true” folk singers and creating a cult of authenticity, according to expectations and valuations of the American roots (Filene 2000). Cecil Sharp (1917) is another good example of an English-American folklorist that embraces the ideas of Herder. Sharp described how he collected and recorded music from the Southern Appalachian Mountains of North America, especially songs and ballads. He exposes the values of a time when the folklorist admired the capacity to sing the songs of the “forefathers” in the mother tongue, with lyrics that reminded him of the elementary literature of what he thought should build the pillars/foundations of the nation. At the same time, he explained the role of the music in education (placing the children of the present generation in possession of the cultural achievements of the past) and as proof of a racial inheritance.

Romantic nationalism is clear in the case of garabato. The upper classes found an inspiration in the most traditional music of the local context, which were adjusted to standards that coincided with the taste of the most priviledge, for example, adding instruments typical of an American big band. In this way they created an element to associate as their own and above all authentically their own, because without a doubt, drum music as played on the Caribbean coast did not exist in Andean cities such as Bogotá or Medellín, where plucked string music was more common. Lo costeño is represented by the instruments, the joy of the music, the openness with American musical styles. Unlike Peña's case, garabato of the upper classes did not compete with the traditional garabato, which was danced at first by the underprivileged classes. In fact, both traditional and stylized garabato were equally appropriate. However, it coincides with Sharp's case on the need for this music to be part of the cultural capital to be taught to future generations of the upper classes.

The appropriation of music did not mean the end of class barriers. In fact, Wade states that Costeño elite was proud to be white and despised the blackness and indigeness of popular sectors (2000). However, eliminating black references means losing the possibility of channeling through music the sexual desire and freedoms that the upper classes could not afford, and the recombination of elements did not imply that black was simply wiped off the map, because it should be always there as a source of creativity, strength and inspiration. It is perhaps for this reason that today we see how the Carnival queen, that is always a member of the elite of Barranquilla, in her public presentations and official videos now includes other musical genres that until very recently were considered in bad taste, such as champeta (Birenbaum 2003; Olímpica 2022).

In this way it can be said that there have been two epochs in which the upper classes "downgraded themselves". They coincide with two moments in which the elites sought to captivate, firstly, the country and now, the world after the declaration of Carnival as part of the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage Lists in 2003. In both cases, advocating Romantic nationalism. These two epochs coincide with the variable relations of Colombian cultures, specifically Caribbean cultures, between local history, which enters conversation at some point with the nation at the beginning of the 20th century and now with the global culture through processes of sedimentation, juxtaposition and intertwining of traditions resulting from a process of hybridization increasingly common (García Canclini 1990). These two epochs also match with key moments in which migration and interaction with other populations begin to mold the festive manifestations, morality patterns, and behaviors inherited to the European tradition of carnival until reaching this moment when globalization turns this celebration into a "mass carnival" in which the festivity begins to become part of the entertainment industry as a spectacle (González 2006).

In other words, the elites had to "downgrade" their estrato in order to adjust to new interests that no longer correspond to those they had in the 20th century when they were looking for a place in the national imaginary. It can be determined that their interest is now to present the carnival within a much broader market where the differential factor, the added value, is again the authenticity and the rescue of the traditional, old friend of nationalist romanticism. This supports what De Oro (2009) says about how the leaders of the Carnival of Barranquilla take advantage of the autochthonous elements to attract consumers, sell the city and take economic advantage of the carnival, which has become a cultural cluster at the service of the dominant sectors. The projection of the Carnival ceased to be regional (Colombian Caribbean Coast) and national (Colombia) to target an international audience that sees the festival as an authentically supranational manifestation (Caribbean Basin) and thus reaffirm the symbolic interests of the elites who, through a discursive project, seek to generate greater influence over the entire population that enjoys the carnival, and influence and connections with countries in the region such as the Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico, Cuba and Trinidad and Tobago where carnivals are also held.

None of this would be possible without the role of the private mass media who from the beginning served to disseminate, position and recreate the discourses on the carnival. In the case of the garabato, these media have been mainly the newspaper El Heraldo and the radio stations La Voz de Barranquilla and Emisora Atlántico, which originated programs about the carnival that began in the first half of the twentieth century (De la Espriella 2003-78). For example, the Garabato del Country parade receives great coverage in the local media where appear reviews, photos, videos, and interviews to the caporales (or captains of the dance troupes) or to some of its members that today has more than 1000 members, accompanied by the Queen of Carnival and the Queen of Colombia (Pardo Donado 2021; De Hoyos 2019).

This essay show how Barranquilla is a quite stratified city not only evidenced by the Colombian system of socioeconomic stratification, but also in part of its main celebrations, the Carnival of Barranquilla. The stratification sought to be only economic to help people, but this is now flawed, and moved to the cultural. Socio-economic barriers have created dynamics of power and symbolic violence, where hierarchies of cultural taste have been created. For this reason, there is music associated to poor people, to rich people, to people of low estrato and people of high estrato. Marx’s class struggle is evident between the different socioeconomic estratos but it was flexible when necessity and context forced the upper classes to do so. Tastes and morality patterns have changed, there has even been room for the appropriation of musical genres from one class to another, and there are reasons to believe that this may happen again in the future.
